Lesson 529.5 — Layered Connectivity Troubleshooting

The written half of this lesson.

This lesson prepares you for finding out why a drop that should be working is not, and for deciding whether the repair is yours to make.

You turn a complaint into questions you can answer, take the readings in an order that saves a walk, and use a cable tester to settle whether the cabling is sound. You then tell the two apart — a path that cannot carry traffic, and a switch that has been told to do something other than what the work order asked for.

The last part is knowing where your share of the job ends. Some faults you correct on the spot. Some belong to whoever owns the addressing, the routing and the equipment beyond your Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN). Handing one of those over, with the readings that show where the fault sits, is a completed job rather than an unfinished one.

  • Cabling Fault or Configuration Fault. The three readings a cable tester gives you, and the four things it cannot answer. Locating a fault by substituting one element at a time. A note on the equipment

Switches differ in ways that affect what you see on the equipment in front of you. Port names differ, so a command shown here may take a different form on your switch; the columns read the same. Indicator conventions differ by manufacturer and by model, so read the legend printed on the chassis before you read the lights. The cable tester described here is a verifier: it reports how the cabling is built and how long it is, and it does not certify a run against a performance standard. Power over Ethernet (PoE) figures, including the class and the watts a port has set aside, are read from the switch rather than from the tester.

529.5.1 Turning a report into something you can test

Lesson 529.5

Layered Connectivity Troubleshooting

A fault report tells you that somebody is unhappy. It does not tell you where to look. This lesson takes you from a complaint to knowing what is wrong and where: what to ask before you touch anything, and which readings separate a cabling fault from a configuration fault. It then covers what you correct yourself, what you hand to somebody else, and how to write the result so that it holds up after you have left the building.

4 sections  ·  11 topics  ·  14 activities

By the end of Lesson 529.5 you will be able to
  • Use ping and traceroute to isolate reachability.
  • Read link and Power over Ethernet (PoE) indicators and switch port status.
  • Confirm physical-layer integrity with a cable tester.
  • Distinguish a cabling fault from a configuration fault.
  • Determine what to fix and what to escalate.
  • Document root cause and remediation.

The first decision on any fault is where to start looking, and you make it from the report.

A report arrives as a sentence. The camera in the loading bay is down. That sentence is a symptom, described by somebody who was not looking at the equipment, and it contains no reading.

Five questions turn it into something you can test.

Five questions to ask before the first reading.
AskWhyWhat the answer changes
Which drop?A room names an area. An outlet identifier names a positionWithout one you are searching a building. With one you are reading the row for that outlet in the cabling records and walking to a known position
What does “down” mean?No lights, lights but no picture, or working and then stopping are three different reportsSeparates a device with no power from a device that is running and cannot be reached. The first sends you to the power and the cabling; the second sends you to the switch
When did it last work?A drop that has never worked and a drop that stopped yesterday have different sets of causesNever worked points at the installation. Stopped yesterday points at whatever changed yesterday
What changed?Somebody moved furniture, worked in the rack, replaced a device, or lost power to the buildingWhatever changed is the first thing to suspect
Is anything else affected?One drop, or every drop served by the same roomIf only that one drop is affected, the fault is on it. If several fail together, the fault is in something they share — the switch, or its link to the rest of the network — and that answer moves the search before you pick up a tool

The five answers tell you where the search is likely to end.

The search itself runs bottom–up through three questions. Is it connected, and is the medium good? Is the port up, in the right Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN), and supplying power? Does the device hold an Internet Protocol (IP) address, and can it reach anything? Anything above those is the network team’s.

You stop at the first question that answers no. What the five questions above change is your expectation of where that no will fall, not the order in which you ask.

A report usually arrives with a diagnosis attached

The network is down. The cable is bad. The switch has failed. Those are guesses made by somebody with no readings, and they are offered in good faith.

Treat the diagnosis as one more symptom rather than as a starting point. A report that names the cable sends you to the cable, and the search then begins one step above where the evidence would have put it.

A fault you cannot make happen

Some reports describe something that is not happening while you are there. Ask when it happens and what else is going on at the time — the answer often describes the cause. A drop that fails every afternoon, or whenever a door is closed, or only when the room fills up, is telling you where to look.

Where you cannot reproduce it, take the readings anyway and record them with the time. A good reading taken during a quiet period is what the next person compares against.

529.5.2 Ping, and the trace command

A ping checks whether a machine at a given IP address is reachable from where you run it. Three different results come back.

A ping to an IP address on your own subnet does not cross a router. A ping to an IP address on a different subnet does. That decides how much a failure tells you: on your own subnet it points at the cabling, the ports and the two devices; across a router it could be anything on the way.

A reply means the device is reachable. Request timed out is a reading rather than a diagnosis: the ping was sent and nothing came back, and that has several causes the ping cannot tell apart.

Destination host unreachable is the third, and it gets misread because it arrives looking like a reply. In the run below, the laptop typing the command holds 10.20.0.10.

ping — run from a laptop at 10.20.0.10
C:\> ping 10.20.0.51

Pinging 10.20.0.51 with 32 bytes of data:
Reply from 10.20.0.10: Destination host unreachable.
Reply from 10.20.0.10: Destination host unreachable.
Reply from 10.20.0.10: Destination host unreachable.
Reply from 10.20.0.10: Destination host unreachable.

Read the IP address the reply came from. 10.20.0.10 is the laptop, not the device, so the laptop is reporting its own failure: it asked the subnet who holds 10.20.0.51 and nothing answered.

Three things produce that, and they are not equally expensive to check. The IP address you are pinging may be wrong — mistyped, or taken from a record that is out of date. Check that first; it costs seconds. Nothing may have answered at that IP address: the device may be off, may never have been installed, may hold a different IP address, or may be unable to answer, and the ping cannot say which. Or the laptop has no route to that subnet — no default gateway, or the wrong mask — and nothing ever left it. The second separates at the switch; the third is on the laptop in front of you.

A reply carrying a router’s IP address rather than the laptop’s means the router could not deliver the ping. That is a different fault and not yours: record the router’s IP address from the reply and hand it over, because it names where delivery stopped.

Open each card. The three results send you to three different places.

The trace command

A trace reports each router the traffic passes through on the way to an IP address, one line per hop, and how long the round trip to each one took. On Windows the command is tracert; on Linux and macOS it is traceroute.

Windows looks up a name for every hop unless you stop it, which slows the trace down and prints names where you wanted IP addresses. -d turns the lookup off. On Linux and macOS the same option is -n.

Inside your own subnet there are no routers to report, so a trace to an IP address there adds nothing to the ping. It is useful where a ping crossed a router and failed: it shows the route the traffic takes, router by router, and how far it got. Some routers along the way will not answer, and the trace still shows the rest.

tracert — a trace that completes
C:\> tracert -d 10.60.0.20

Tracing route to 10.60.0.20 over a maximum of 30 hops

  1     1 ms     1 ms     1 ms  10.20.0.1
  2     2 ms     1 ms     2 ms  10.0.0.1
  3     *        *        *     Request timed out.
  4     3 ms     2 ms     3 ms  10.60.0.20

Trace complete.

Four hops, and the last one is the IP address you asked for, so the path works end to end. Hop 1 is the router the laptop uses to reach other networks. Hop 3 answered nothing and forwarded the traffic anyway — commonly a router set not to reply, or one limiting how often it answers; either way it is not a fault. The last line, reading Trace complete., is what tells you so. Each hop is probed three times, which is why there are three figures on a line.

tracert — a trace that stops at the first router
C:\> tracert -d 10.60.0.20

Tracing route to 10.60.0.20 over a maximum of 30 hops

  1     1 ms     1 ms     1 ms  10.20.0.1
  2     *        *        *     Request timed out.
  3     *        *        *     Request timed out.

The first router answered and nothing past it did. The command keeps trying up to thirty hops unless you stop it with Ctrl+C. Record that the first router answered and nothing beyond it did: evidence for whoever owns the routers, and not a fault you can act on.

A row of stars is not a fault

Hop 3 in the first trace is a router that does not answer and forwards normally. Stars in the middle of a trace that completes have found nothing wrong.

The useful reading is the point where a trace stops and does not resume, taken together with a ping to the same IP address.

529.5.3 Indicators, and what the switch says instead

An indicator is the fastest reading on the job and the least specific. It tells you that something is happening. The switch tells you what.

Two things limit what an indicator is worth: what its colors mean is not standardized, and a light at one end says little about the other.

Indicator conventions are not standard

What green, amber and flashing mean varies by manufacturer, by model, and on many switches by which mode the front panel is switched to — the same indicator shows link state in one mode and power delivery in another.

Read the legend printed on the chassis before you read the lights, and record what the legend says rather than what you assumed. On the device end, the startup sequence is in the device documentation; read it once for the device you install most often.

Two indications in particular point somewhere other than where they seem to.

  • Lit at the switch, nothing at the device. The link is up at the switch end, so the fault is past it: the path, the device, or the possibility that the lit port is not the one serving this drop. A lit port several floors away from a dark device does not tell you which.
  • Steady at both ends, and the user still reporting a fault. The path carries a link, and nothing above a link has been checked: the speed it settled at, the VLAN the port is in, the IP address the device holds, and whether anything can reach it.
A power indicator reports the port, not the device

A device that is receiving power and cannot run properly on it produces the same indication at the switch as one that is running normally. The device’s own behavior is the reading for that, and on a new install it is often the only one available.

What the switch says

The switch answers the same questions in words, in the same few seconds, and without ambiguity. Here the columns are read across five ports, four of which have something wrong.

show interfaces status — five ports, four problems
Switch# show interfaces status

Port      Name               Status       Vlan       Duplex  Speed Type
Gi1/0/11                     notconnect   20           auto   auto 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/12                     connected    20         a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/13                     disabled     20           auto   auto 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/14                     connected    1          a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/15                     connected    20         a-full  a-100 10/100/1000BaseTX
  • Gi1/0/11 — enabled, and no link is forming. Nothing plugged in, a cord not seated, a broken path, or a device that is off.
  • Gi1/0/12 — the one that is right.
  • Gi1/0/13 — shut down in the configuration. Nothing on the far end will change that.
  • Gi1/0/14 — a sound link, sitting in VLAN 1. The port was never moved into the group the work order named, so the device will start normally and reach nothing it expects.
  • Gi1/0/15 — a sound link at 100 Mbps, where a gigabit device on a gigabit port should give 1000.

Port names differ between switches; where a switch names its ports Fa1/0/12 rather than Gi1/0/12, the columns read the same.

Power is reported by show power inline, read for the same port.

Which reading to take first

A managed switch is usually reachable over the network, so both readings can be taken from wherever you are sitting. The indicators cannot: they are at the two ends of the drop, and those may be on different floors.

Take the switch readings first. They name the port rather than describing a light.

529.5.4 What a cable tester answers, and what it does not

A cable tester tells you three things about a length of cable.

  • The wire map. Whether every conductor reaches the right pin at the far end. It names opens, shorts, single-wire faults, reversed and crossed pairs, and split pairs. It also reports which of the tester’s far-end adapters is plugged in at the other end. Where a set of numbered adapters is available, that is how one cable is told from another in a bundle.
  • The length. The length of each pair, and the distance to a fault where there is one.
  • What is at the far end. Whether the cable is plugged into an Ethernet port and what speed that port advertises — 10, 100 or 1000 Mbps — whether telephone voltage is present, and whether Power over Ethernet is present and on which pairs.
The faults a wire map names, and how each one behaves in service.
Named asWhat is wrongHow it behaves in service
OpenA conductor does not reach the far end — a broken wire, a pin not seated in the plug, a conductor not punched downCommonly no link at all. An open on the 4/5 or 7/8 pairs can leave a 100 Mbps link working, and can stop the power where the port sends its current on those pairs rather than on 1/2 and 3/6
ShortTwo conductors touching, usually a plug crimped over stripped copper or a crushed cableNo link. A powered port may refuse to supply power rather than energize a short, so the device stays dark and the port reports nothing flowing
Reversed pairThe two conductors of one pair swapped at one endFrequently carries traffic without complaint. The wire map names it and service does not, which is why it survives in installed cabling for years
Crossed pairsA whole pair terminated in the wrong position at one endDepends which pairs moved. Commonly no link, and where a link does form it is unreliable
Split pairEvery conductor reaches the right pin, but two wires from different twisted pairs have been used together as a pairThe one that hides. Continuity is correct end to end, so a simple continuity tester passes it. It commonly links at 100 Mbps and fails or produces errors at 1000

The split pair

A split pair is wired to the right pins and twisted into the wrong pairs. Every conductor goes where the diagram says, so anything that tests continuity conductor by conductor reports it as good. What has gone is the twisting that cancels interference between the two wires of a pair, and that shows only once the link is asked to run faster. The tester finds it by checking the pairing itself rather than the conductors one at a time; a plain continuity tester cannot.

100 Mbps uses the 1/2 and 3/6 pairs and leaves the other two alone. 1000 Mbps uses all four. So a split on the pairs the lower speed does not touch can sit in a drop for years, carrying traffic, until somebody fits a faster device. The wire map names it directly, and that separates a damaged path from a device that was only ever going to run at 100 Mbps.

The length reading

A permanent link is limited to 90 meters and a channel to 100 meters. A run past those limits may work, may work slowly, and may work until something else on the path changes. Nothing about the drop announces it and no indicator reports it — the only way to know is to read the length.

A tester reporting 118 meters on a channel has found a design fault rather than a break, and the remedy is a different route or equipment closer to the room. Neither of those is a cord swap, which is why the number goes in the record and the job goes to whoever decides where the cabling runs.

Where the length reads much shorter than the run should be, or differs sharply between pairs, the tester is reporting the distance to a fault rather than the length of the cable. The distance it reports tells you whether to look at the plug in your hand or at something between the two ends.

Two test modes

The wire map test needs both ends of the cable free, with the tester on one end and its far-end adapter on the other. Nothing else can be plugged in.

The service test needs the far end plugged into a live port. That is how the tester reports the port speed and whether power is present.

You cannot take both readings at once. Take the one your evidence points at first, and take the other only if the first comes back clean.

Four things it does not answer

  • It does not measure insertion loss or crosstalk, so it cannot say a run meets a category. A cable tester confirms that the cabling is built correctly; certifying a link against a performance standard is a different instrument and a different job.
  • It does not report the Power over Ethernet class. It reports that power is present and which pairs carry it. The class and the watts set aside come from the switch.
  • It knows nothing about the switch configuration. A port shut down, a port in the wrong VLAN, and a port set never to supply power all test perfectly on a cable tester, because none of them is a fault in the cabling.
  • It reports only on what you plugged it into. A clean test on a cord says nothing about the rest of the path.
Testing a cord, and testing the run

The two patch cords are the parts you can lift out and test on a bench with both ends free. Each takes under a minute, and between them they either clear the two elements most likely to have failed or find the fault.

The run between the outlet and the patch panel cannot be tested that way, and testing it is a separate task. Where the evidence points at that run, say so and schedule the test, rather than recording a cord problem that the cord swap did not fix.

529.5.5 Isolating by substitution

Swapping one element for a known-good one locates a fault in a path you cannot see into. It works on one condition: you change one thing at a time.

The path from a device to a switch port has seven elements and two of them are cords. Those two are the parts a person handles, so they are the ones that fail most, and they are also the only two that can be exchanged in seconds without tools.

The two cords, the device and the switch port are drawn in blue; the outlet, horizontal cable and panel are drawn in gray.

Four of the boxes carry a marker bar underneath with a number. Marker 1 sits under the cord from the device to the outlet, marker 2 under the cord from the panel to the switch, marker 3 under the switch port, and marker 4 under the device. A line beneath reads: one change at a time, and put back what was not the fault.

The list below gives each substitution. One, a known-good cord at the device end, clears that cord. Two, a known-good cord at the panel end, clears that cord. Three, a second switch port configured the same way, clears the port, its settings and its power. Four, a laptop in place of the device, clears the device and proves the path as far as the switch. A closing line reads: the three unmarked elements — outlet, horizontal cable, panel termination — cannot be substituted; they are what remains.

SubstitutionA good result clearsIt does not clear

Four rules

  • One change at a time. Change two things, get a working drop, and you know the fault was in one of two places without knowing which. Undo one of them and retest.
  • Put back what was not the fault. A trail of substituted cords left in a rack becomes somebody else’s fault report next month, and nothing will connect it back to this visit.
  • Keep and label the part that failed. A cord that fails a wire map is evidence while the job is open and rubbish afterward. Unmarked, it goes back in the bag and reaches another drop.
  • Write down each swap as you make it. The record needs the sequence, and the sequence is the first thing memory loses.
A substitution locates a fault; it does not explain one

“The cord was bad” is a location. What was wrong with it — a latch snapped off, a section crushed under a chair leg, a plug that was never fully crimped — is what stops the same fault appearing on the same desk in three months.

The tester answers that in seconds on a cord already in your hand, and the record needs that answer.

Known-good means tested, not new

A cord out of a bag is unverified. A cord that has been in a toolkit for a year and has been stood on is unverified. Known-good means it has passed a wire map on the tester and has been marked as the one kept for this purpose.

Substituting a faulty cord for a faulty cord produces a reading that says the fault is somewhere else, and sends the rest of the job in the wrong direction.

When the substitutions run out

Where every cord and every switch port has been substituted and the fault stays with the drop, what is left is the permanent link: the outlet, the horizontal cable, and the panel termination.

Testing that is its own task, with both ends unpatched and the drop out of service while it runs. Record that the cords and the port were cleared, and by what reading.

Lesson 529.5 · Section 2 Cabling Fault or Configuration Fault

529.5.6 Telling a broken path from a wrong setting

Almost every fault on a drop is one of two things: a path that cannot carry the traffic, or a switch told to do the wrong thing. One reading usually tells you which.

A cabling fault is a path that cannot carry the traffic asked of it: a conductor that does not reach, a split pair, a plug not seated, a run past its length limit.

A configuration fault is different: the path carries traffic, and the switch has been told to do something other than what the work order asked for. That covers a port shut down, a port left in the group it started in, a port set never to supply power, and a speed or duplex forced at one end.

A cord in the wrong panel position belongs to neither: the path carries traffic and delivers it to the wrong outlet.

From the desk the two look identical, so the report cannot separate them. They separate at the switch, and usually in one column.

Two readings settle most cases. A clean wire map with a Status column reading disabled is a configuration fault. A wire map naming an open with a Status column reading notconnect is a cabling fault. The awkward cases are the ones where both readings look reasonable, and those are the next topic.

Match each reading to what it names.

529.5.7 Faults that look like each other

Five pairs of causes that arrive as the same complaint. Each pair separates on one reading.

Open each card. The two causes in each pair produce the same report and take different repairs.

Take the reading that tells the pair apart before you make the repair

You can settle any of these pairs by trying the repair for one of them and seeing whether the fault goes away. That works, and it produces a record that says what was done and cannot say what was wrong.

One reading first, then one repair, gives you both.

Lesson 529.5 · Section 3 Fix, Escalate, Record

529.5.8 What you fix, and what you hand over

Some faults stop being yours before they are fixed. That is not a failure, and what you hand over decides whether the next person can use it.

Two questions decide whether a fault is yours. Is it in the cabling or the port, and are you authorized to change it? The first is technical and the second is a site rule, and both have to answer yes.

Where the boundary falls on the faults you will meet most.
The faultYours?What you do
A cord with a wire map faultYesReplace it, keep and label the faulty one, retest, record what the tester named
A cord not fully seatedYesSeat it, confirm the link came up, record it as found rather than quietly correcting it
A cord in the wrong panel position or the wrong switch portYesRepatch to the position the work order gives, confirm at both ends, record the position it was found in
A port shut downWhere you are authorizedRe-enable it, confirm the link, record it. Where you are not, report it with the reading that names it
A port in a VLAN other than the one specifiedWhere you are authorizedThe same. The reading is the Vlan column, and it goes in the record either way
A port set never to supply powerWhere you are authorizedThe same
A device that is powered, linked, in the right VLAN and heard by the switch, and still does not answerNoHand it over with every reading you took
Addressing, routing, a rule that blocks traffic, a server, a management systemNoHand it over. None of it is readable from where you are standing
Anything on equipment you were not asked to work onNoReport it. Do not touch it
Authority is a site question, not a technical one

On some sites a technician corrects a wrong VLAN during the same visit and notes it. On others every configuration change goes through a change process, and making the correction without one is treated more seriously than the fault was.

Settle which it is before you start, along with where the record lives and whether you may write to it. A correction you were not authorized to make is a second fault report, this one with your name on it.

An escalation done properly is a completed job

A fault you have shown to be outside the cabling and the port, handed over with the readings that put it there, is finished work.

A technician who keeps going past their boundary spends the afternoon on a cause they cannot reach, and the fault arrives at the team that owns it hours later with nothing attached to it. In that situation the hand-over is the job, and it is finished at the point where the readings stop being yours to take.

What makes a hand-over usable

A hand-over carries three things: what you verified, what you found, and where you stopped. On a fault those are not enough. It needs a fourth.

What you ruled out, and by what reading. The next person’s first instinct is to suspect the cabling, because that is where most faults live. Give them the reading that makes checking it unnecessary, and they start where you finished instead of where you began.

A hand-over written from readings

Camera at outlet 2A-09 does not answer. Cord to the device replaced with a tested cord, no change. Panel position B/09 repatched to a second port configured the same way, no change. Wire map clean on both cords. Switch port reads connected, VLAN 20, a-full a-1000, class 3 with 15.4 W set aside, Oper on. The camera’s MAC address is learned on the port. A ping from a laptop in VLAN 20 times out. Cabling, port and power are ruled out by the readings above. Handing over at the device and its addressing.

Four things not to touch
  • An uplink between switches, or between a switch and anything else you were not asked to work on.
  • A port serving somebody who has not reported a fault.
  • A configuration you were not asked to change, even where you can see it is wrong. Report it.
  • Equipment belonging to another trade.

When the pattern is bigger than the drop

Where every drop served by one switch stops at the same time, the fault is not on any of the drops. Read one of them to prove the drop is sound, then report the pattern and the time it started.

A fault common to twenty ports belongs to whoever owns the switch and its links, and substituting cords on a drop that has already read good tests something proven.

529.5.9 Writing the root cause and the remediation

A fault record has five fields. Two of them are usually written as opinions, and both can be written as facts instead.

The five fields of a fault record.
FieldWhat goes in it
Symptom as reportedThe words you were given, and the drop they were reported against. Not your interpretation of them
What you foundThe readings, with their values, in the order you took them
Root causeThe thing that, once changed, made the symptom stop
RemediationWhat you changed, and what you put back
VerificationThe reading you took after the repair, with its value

A root cause is not a restated symptom

The same four faults, written as an opinion and written as a cause.
Written as an opinionWritten as a cause
Cable problemOpen on conductor 6 in the cord between the outlet and the device, 1.2 m from the device end, named by the tester’s wire map
Fixed the portPort Gi1/0/13 was shut down in the configuration. The Status column read disabled

User errorThe panel cord was in position B/15 rather than B/14, so the switch port was serving a different outlet

Read a root cause back and ask whether somebody could produce the same fault from it. If they could, it is a cause. If they could only recognize it once it happened again, it is a symptom in different words.

Remediation

Name what you changed and name what you returned. A record saying that a cord was replaced, without saying that the second cord was put back, leaves the next technician looking for a change that is no longer there.

Then re-take the reading that was bad and write the value down. A repair without an after-reading is a claim. Where the bad reading was a ping timing out, the after-reading is a ping replying. Where it was Oper off, the after-reading is Oper on with a class and a figure beside it.

Two records that are complete without a repair

  • No fault found. Write what you tested and what each reading said, with the time. A drop that reads good is a result, and the report probably describes something real that was not happening while you were there.
  • Outside scope, handed over. The readings, the list of what they rule out, where you stopped, who it went to and when.
Write it at the rack

A record written at the end of the day is written from memory, and values are the first thing memory drops. A port number becomes “port twelve or thirteen” and a wattage disappears altogether.

A reading nobody wrote down cannot be quoted later.

529.5.10 Two faults, worked through

The same sequence, run twice. One ends in a repair and one ends in a hand-over.

One — a desk phone that will not power up

The report. The phone at desk 3B-07 has no lights. It worked on Friday.

The questions. One drop, not several. It worked three days ago. What changed is that the desk was moved over the weekend.

The first reading, taken at the switch before walking anywhere.

show interfaces status — the port for outlet 3B-07
Switch# show interfaces status

Port      Name               Status       Vlan       Duplex  Speed Type
Gi1/0/22                     notconnect   20           auto   auto 10/100/1000BaseTX

Enabled, and no link forming. The Status column reads notconnect rather than disabled, so the port is not shut down; and a wrong VLAN would still let a link form, so that is not the cause of this report either. The search is now on the path and the device.

show power inline — the same port
Switch# show power inline Gi1/0/22

Interface Admin  Oper       Power   Device              Class Max
                            (Watts)
--------- ------ ---------- ------- ------------------- ----- ----
Gi1/0/22  auto   off        0.0     n/a                 n/a   30.0

Power is permitted and nothing is flowing, so nothing was detected on the far end. Run without naming a port, the same command prints the pool on its header line, and there it showed room left. Between them the two readings rule out the power setting and the pool, and leave a broken path or a device that is not connected.

The substitutions, one at a time. The cord from the desk to the outlet is replaced with a tested cord: no change. That cord is put back on the bench for a wire map rather than back on the desk. The panel cord is replaced with a tested cord: the phone lights up.

The reading on the part that failed. The original panel cord is tested. The wire map names a short between two conductors, 0.4 m from one plug — the end that had been folded against the side of the rack.

The verification. The port reads connected, VLAN 20, a-full a-1000, class 2 with 7.0 W set aside, Oper on. The phone’s MAC address appears on the port. A ping from a laptop in VLAN 20 gets four replies.

The record. Root cause: short between two conductors in the panel cord at the switch end, 0.4 m from the plug, named by the wire map. Remediation: cord replaced with a tested 1 m cord, routed clear of the rack edge; the faulty cord is labeled and held; the desk cord was replaced during testing and put back. Verification as above, with the time and initials.

What the first two readings did

Neither of them found the fault. Together they took the switch configuration, the power settings and the power pool out of the job in under a minute, so the substitutions that followed were aimed at the two elements still in question rather than at everything on the path.

Two — three drops that stop together

The report. Three access points on the second floor stopped this morning. All three are served by the same telecommunications room.

The questions. Not one drop but three. All three worked yesterday. All three stopped within the same few minutes. Nothing was worked on in the ceiling.

The readings, taken on one of the three. The port reads connected, VLAN 20, a-full a-1000. Power reads Admin auto, Oper on, class 3 with 15.4 W set aside. The access point’s MAC address is learned on the port. A ping from a laptop patched into VLAN 20 on the same switch gets four replies from the access point.

What that combination says. The drop is sound and the device is running and reachable from inside its own VLAN. Whatever has failed is not on this drop, and the same readings on the other two agree.

The reading that names the boundary.

tracert — from the laptop in VLAN 20, toward a system in another VLAN
C:\> tracert 10.60.0.20

Tracing route to 10.60.0.20 over a maximum of 30 hops

  1     *        *        *     Request timed out.
  2     *        *        *     Request timed out.

Devices inside VLAN 20 reach each other, and nothing outside it answers — not even the first router. Stopped with Ctrl+C after two hops. The first hop failing to answer was the reading.

The hand-over. Three drops, all reading sound at the port, all powered, all heard by the switch, all reachable from a laptop inside their own VLAN. Nothing outside the VLAN answers, from any of them, and all three stopped within the same few minutes. Cabling, ports and power are ruled out by the readings. Handing over at the switch’s connection to the rest of the network.

The record. Root cause: not established, and outside the cabling and the ports. What was ruled out, by which reading, and the time each was taken. Where it went, and to whom.

Two jobs, two records, both closed

One names a cause and a repair. The other names a boundary and the readings that put it there.

Lesson 529.5 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

529.5.11 What you covered

Turn the report into questions, take the readings in order, tell the two apart, repair what is yours, hand over the rest with evidence, and write it down while you are still at the rack.

A report is a symptom described by somebody who was not looking at the equipment. Five questions turn it into something testable: which drop, what “down” means, when it last worked, what changed, and whether anything else is affected. One drop affected puts the fault on that drop; several failing together put it in something they share. A diagnosis arriving with the report is one more symptom, not a starting point.

A ping checks whether a machine at a given IP address is reachable from where you run it, and three different results come back. A ping to an IP address on your own subnet does not cross a router and a ping to another subnet does, which decides how much a failure tells you. A reply proves the device is reachable. A timeout is a reading rather than a diagnosis. A reply carrying your own IP address and the words destination host unreachable means nothing on the subnet answered to that IP address. Either the IP address you are pinging is wrong, which is the cheaper thing to check, or nothing answered at it and the ping cannot say why. The trace command shows the route the traffic takes, router by router: tracert -d on Windows, traceroute -n on Linux and macOS, the option stopping it from looking up a name for every hop. It adds nothing inside your own subnet and is useful where a ping across a router has failed. Some routers will not answer, and a row of stars in a trace that completes is not a fault.

Indicators are the fastest reading and the least specific, and their conventions differ by manufacturer, by model and by front-panel mode, so read the legend on the chassis before you read the lights. A power indicator says the port is supplying power, not that the device is running properly on it. The switch answers the same questions in words. In show interfaces status, the Status column separates notconnect from disabled, the Vlan column names the group the port is in, and the Speed and Duplex columns say how the link settled.

A cable tester tells you three things about a length of cable. The wire map names opens, shorts, reversed and crossed pairs, and split pairs. The length reading gives the length of each pair and the distance to a fault, against a 90 meter permanent link and a 100 meter channel — a limit no symptom announces. The service reading reports the port speed advertised at the far end, telephone voltage, and whether PoE is present and on which pairs. It does not measure insertion loss or crosstalk, so it cannot certify a run against a performance standard; it does not report the PoE class, which comes from the switch; and it cannot see a configuration at all. The wire map needs both ends free, the service reading needs the far end live, and you cannot take both at once.

Substitution locates a fault in a path you cannot see into. Change one thing at a time, put back what was not the fault, keep and label the part that failed, and write down each swap as you make it. A known-good cord is one that has passed a wire map, not one out of a bag. A substitution locates a fault and does not explain it — the tester does that, on the part in your hand. Where every cord and every port has been cleared and the fault stays with the drop, the permanent link is what remains, and testing it is its own task with the drop out of service.

Five pairs of causes arrive as the same complaint and separate on one reading each. A port shut down against a dead run, on the Status column of show interfaces status. A port set never to supply power against a pool with nothing left, on the Admin column and header line of show power inline. A cord in the wrong panel position against a dead switch port, on what the switch port reports — a link where there should not be one is as much a reading as no link where there should be. A wrong VLAN against a device with no working IP address, on the Vlan column of show interfaces status. A split pair against a device that only does 100 Mbps, on the wire map, because the switch reports the speed and cannot say why.

Two questions decide whether a fault is yours: is it in the cabling or the port, and are you authorized to change it. Cords, seating and patching are yours. A port shut down, a wrong VLAN and a port set never to supply power are yours where the site allows you to configure that switch, and a reading to report where it does not. Addressing, routing, blocking rules, servers and other people’s equipment are not yours at all.

Escalation is a pass, not a failure

The fourth is what stops the next person starting at the cabling.
