Lesson 529.6 — Port Map, As-Built, and Hand-Off

What a finished job leaves behind, and how you check it before you leave.

Six months from now, another technician walks into room 2A with a work order that names the desk in the corner and the access point in the ceiling above it. They have never been in this building. Which switch port serves that desk? Which ceiling tile is the access point fixed to? Was the cable to either one ever tested, and by whom? What they can answer in the next five minutes is whatever the paperwork says.

Three questions, three documents. The port map says which switch port serves each position. The as-built says which room each device is in, where in that room, and how it is fixed in place. The test record says what each drop measured, on what day, with which tool, and who was holding the tester.

A record can be right on paper and wrong about the room. So all three get checked against the room before they leave your hands, and then go to a named person on a named day rather than into a folder somewhere.

One topic covers doing the writing with an Artificial Intelligence (AI) chat tool: what it does well, what it invents, and what a site will and will not let you paste into it.

How the lesson is organized

  • The Records You Leave Behind. The three documents, one at a time — what goes in each, and what a test record is not allowed to claim.
  • Proving the Records and Handing Them Over. Checking the labels in both directions, the AI chat tool, reading the switch, and what goes in the set you hand over.
  • Wrapping Up. The whole lesson in one place.

A note on the standards and the equipment

ANSI/TIA-606-C is the standard behind this, from the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA). It sets out how an identifier is built and what a record has to carry, and leaves the site to choose the values. It requires the site to have a written scheme and to keep the labels and the records in step. Find that scheme before you read any of its labels.

Equipment differs. Port names vary between switches, so a port called Gi1/0/12 here may be Fa1/0/12 on yours; the columns the switch prints are the same. And a cable tester that reports a wire map and a length is not measuring performance — the Power over Ethernet (PoE) class, with the watts a port has set aside, comes from the switch.

529.6.1 What the next technician inherits

Lesson 529.6

Port Map, As-Built, and Hand-Off

The cabling and the ports stay in the building. So does the paperwork, and it is what somebody reads first on the next visit. This lesson covers the three records you write at the end of a job. It then takes you through checking the labels on the hardware against what you wrote, checking what you wrote against what is in the room, and handing it all to a named person.

3 sections  ·  9 topics  ·  12 activities

By the end of Lesson 529.6 you will be able to
  • Record port-to-device assignments.
  • Map device locations onto the as-built.
  • Cross-check cable labels against port records.
  • Record verification and test results.
  • Generate an Artificial Intelligence (AI)-assisted port map and as-built.
  • Verify the documentation against the physical install.

Three records answer three different questions. Hand over two of them and you have left a question nobody can answer.

An install is finished when somebody else can work on it without telephoning you. Three records answer what they would otherwise have to ask.

The three records, and the question each one answers.
RecordThe question it answersWho reads it
Port mapWhich switch port serves this outlet, and what is on the end of itWhoever takes the next fault on the drop, or moves a device to a different port
As-builtWhere the outlets and the devices are in the buildingWhoever has to find an outlet or a device, and whoever plans work in the same space
Test recordWhat each drop measured, when, with which tool, and who took the readingWhoever is asked whether the work was done, and to what standard

Open each card.

The three are joined by the identifier. One drop carries the same identifier on its faceplate, in its port map row, beside its symbol on the as-built, and on its line in the test record. Where that identifier differs between two of them, a reader cannot move from one record to the other.

ANSI/TIA-606-C, published by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA), covers identifiers, labels, records and drawings as one subject rather than as separate jobs. It sets out how an identifier is built and leaves the values to the site. It requires the site to have a scheme, to write it down, and to keep the labels and the records in step with each other. How much administration a site needs is scaled to its size, from a single telecommunications room up to several buildings. What that means on the day: before you read a label or write one, find the scheme it was written to.

A record nobody can find is not a record

Each of the three sits somewhere: a spreadsheet on a shared drive, a drawing in a document system, a printed sheet in a sleeve inside the telecommunications room door, or a record in a cable management system.

Find out where all three live before you start writing to any of them, and find out which of them you are allowed to change. A good record written into the wrong file leaves the site with two records that disagree.

529.6.2 A port map for a room, not for one drop

A port map for a whole room can be wrong in ways one row cannot — two rows claiming the same switch port, or a position with no row at all.

You worked on four positions this week. The port map you hand over covers every position the panel terminates, because it belongs to the telecommunications room and not to your job. That includes the positions with nothing patched to them.

A position with no row looks like a position that does not exist

A spare panel port left out of the port map is invisible to whoever is asked next week to bring up a new desk. They will order a new run rather than patch the one already terminated three feet away.

Write the spares in, marked as spare. A port map row that says terminated, not patched is a usable answer.

What a room-level map needs that one row does not

Three columns that only make sense across a whole room.
ColumnWhat it holdsWhat it prevents
StateIn service; spare; reserved, meaning held for work already planned; or retiredA blank cell being read as either a spare or an omission, with no way to tell which
Last changedThe day the row was last written toTwo people disagreeing about which of two arrangements is the current one
Test referenceThe line or file that holds the readings for that dropA reader who can see the drop exists and cannot find out whether it was ever tested

Three rules for the set

  • No two rows may name the same switch port. A port serves one position at a time. Two rows naming Gi1/0/12 means one of them is out of date, and the map cannot say which.
  • A position taken out of service keeps its row. Change the state to retired and date it. Deleting the row hides a terminated cable sitting behind that panel position.
  • Write down the old value before you type the new one. A drop repatched from Gi1/0/18 to Gi1/0/31 leaves no trace of where it was if the cell is retyped and nothing more is written. Where the site keeps a change log, the old value goes there; where it does not, it goes in writing with the job paperwork.

Row 2B-05 is terminated and no longer to be used — its outlet went when a partition wall came out, and the run now dead-ends above the ceiling. The row stays so that nobody patches it expecting a working drop.

Write it down at the drop, not at the end of the day

Change the cord, then write the change down before you move on — into the port map if you can reach it, into your notes if you cannot. Either is a record made while you are standing there.

What does not work is reconstructing the port map row afterwards from memory. Port numbers are the first thing memory blurs.

529.6.3 Writing a device location somebody can walk to

A room number gets somebody into the room. A device on a suspended ceiling can be at any one of dozens of tiles.

The port map tells you which port. It does not tell you where to stand. The location goes on the as-built, and it goes there in four parts.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram is in two halves.

On the left is a plan view of Room 2A, drawn as a rectangle with faint lines across it showing a suspended ceiling grid. The top edge is labeled north wall. A blue circle marked AP sits in a grid square in the middle right of the room, with the label tile C-2 beside it. On the lower wall, an orange rectangle marks an outlet, labeled 2A-14, with the word outlet beside it.

On the right, under the heading The Location Entry, four boxes give the four parts of the entry and the value each one holds for this device.

Space: Room 2A, second floor.

Position in the space: Ceiling tile C-2, 3.2 meters from the north wall.

Mount: Clipped to the ceiling grid, reached from below.

Facing: Not directional, recorded as not applicable.

The four parts

  • The space. The room number or the name on the door, and the floor. Where the building numbers its rooms differently from the way its occupants name them, write the number and put the name beside it.
  • The position within the space. Measured from something that will still be there next year. A tile reference where the ceiling has a grid, otherwise a distance from a named wall or a corner. Above the third desk is wrong the week the desks move.
  • The mount. Ceiling tile, wall bracket, pole, fixed to the structural slab above the suspended ceiling, or inside a cabinet. This decides what the next visit has to bring and whether it needs a second person.
  • Which way it faces, for anything directional. A camera and a directional antenna both point somewhere, and a replacement fitted the other way round is a fault report. Where the device is not directional, write that rather than leaving the field empty.
Putting it on the drawing

A device gets a symbol at its position, with the identifier from its port map row printed beside it. A symbol drawn with no identifier shows that something is there and cannot be tied to any other record.

The as-built itself carries a date and a revision mark. A drawing with no date and no revision mark cannot be told apart from the one it replaced, and both will be in the folder.

Two locations, and they are not the same location

A device and the outlet it is patched to are at two different places, and on a ceiling device they can be several meters apart.

Record both. Somebody sent to move the device needs the first; somebody sent to test the run needs the second.

529.6.4 The test record, and what it is allowed to claim

A test record is a reading with a date, a tool and a name attached. Leave out any of the three and the reading can be argued with.

Two years from now somebody asks whether the drops in room 2A were ever tested. The answer is a line in a file, with a date on it, or the answer is a shrug. A port map row says a drop exists and where it goes. The test record says what happened when somebody put a tester on it.

What one line of a test record carries.
FieldWhat goes in itWhy a reader needs it
DropThe outlet identifier, and the panel position it lands onTies the reading to a position rather than to a room
What was measuredWire map, length, port speed advertised at the far end, power present — each named rather than lumped together as a testDifferent tools answer different questions, and the word test alone does not say which was asked
ResultThe value where there is one, and pass or fail where the tool gives a verdictA length of 74 m can be compared with a limit. The word good cannot
ToolThe make and model, and the serial number where the site asks for itIt decides what the result covers, and it is among the first things questioned if a reading is later disputed
Date and timeWhen the reading was takenThe reading describes the drop on that day and no other
Taken byThe name or initials of whoever held the toolA record nobody signed cannot be asked about

Verified is not certified

These two words describe different jobs, and using the second where you did the first is a common way a technician overstates a record.

What each kind of tool measures, and what its record may say.
A verifierA certifier
What it readsHow the cabling is wired and how long it is: the wire map and the length. It also reports whether power is present and the speed advertised at the far endHow the cabling performs: insertion loss, crosstalk and the other measurements that define a performance category
What its record may sayThe run is wired correctly, is within length, and its far end advertised 1000 Mbps on the day of the testThe run meets a named performance category, measured either as the fixed cabling on its own or as the whole path including the cords at each end
What its record may not sayThat the run meets a performance category. The tool never measured one—

Where a job requires certification, it is a separate task with a different tool and a report of its own. A wire map fault found by a verifier is a fault. A clean wire map is not proof that the run performs.

Do not write a verdict the tool did not give

Writing Certified to Category 6A from a wire map reading claims performance the tool never measured. Lines like that get quoted back years later.

Write what the tool reported, and name it. Where somebody wants a category on the record, that is a request for a certification test.

The readings that go with a powered drop

Cabling readings come from the cable tester. The four readings below describe the port and the device: three of them you read off the switch, and the fourth is a ping you run from a machine. Each bullet names the command that produced it.

  • Link. The Status, Duplex and Speed columns of show interfaces status, and the Vlan column from the same output.
  • Power. The Admin, Oper, Class and Power columns of show power inline — where Power is what the port has set aside for the device rather than a measurement of what the device is drawing.
  • Presence on the port. Whether show mac address-table interface lists a Media Access Control (MAC) address for that port.
  • Reachability. A ping to the device from a machine in the same VLAN, recorded as sent and received counts.

The tester overlaps with some of this — it also reports whether power is present and what speed the far end advertised. Where you can reach the switch, the switch answer is the fuller one, because it gives the class and the watts as well.

Record the column name and the value, rather than a summary. Oper on, class 3, 15.4 W set aside can be compared with a later reading. Power fine cannot.

529.6.5 Checking every label against every row

Checking one port map row means finding four labels in two places, which is why this is a scheduled task rather than a glance at the rack.

An identifier appears on the hardware in more than one place: the faceplate at the work area, both ends of the horizontal cable — the fixed run between the outlet and the panel — and the panel position. Two of those are behind something — the cable label at the outlet end sits inside the box behind the faceplate, and the label at the panel end sits in the bundle behind the panel. The switch port carries no label at all and is read from the switch.

Text description of this diagram

At the top of the diagram sits one port map row, holding the outlet identifier 2A-14, the panel position B slash 14, the switch port Gi1/0/12, and the description Access point, room 2A.

Five lines fan down from that row to five boxes, each box being a place the row can be checked.

Faceplate at the outlet: 2A-14. Cable label, outlet end: 2A-14. Panel position label: B slash 14. Cable label, panel end: 2A-14. The fifth box, drawn with a dashed border and reached by a dashed line, reads: Switch port, no label, read at the switch.

Below sit two boxes giving the two directions a sweep can run.

Record to hardware: take each row and find its labels. Finds hardware missing, unlabeled or wrong.

Hardware to record: read every label and find the row it belongs to. Finds a label with no row.

Run it in both directions

The two directions find different defects, and a sweep run one way leaves the other kind of defect standing.

  • Record to hardware. Take each row in turn and find the labels it names. This finds a row whose faceplate is blank, whose cable label has fallen off, or whose panel position is labeled as something else.
  • Hardware to record. Go back through both places, reading every label you can see at the outlets and at the panel, and find the row each one belongs to. This finds hardware nobody wrote into the port map: an outlet installed during a later fit-out, a panel position labeled and never written down, a device somebody added on a Friday.

Start from the port map, because it is a finite list and it tells you where to walk. Then take the second direction on the way back through the room.

What you write as you go

All of this goes on one sheet, the check list, headed by the check itself: its date, what it covered, and who made it. A row that agrees is ticked off there, and not dated in the port map — the port map’s Last changed column records changes, and a check is not a change. A row that does not agree gets an exception line on the same sheet, and an exception line has four parts.

The four parts of an exception line.
PartExample
The positionOutlet 2A-12, panel position B / 12
What the record saysFaceplate label 2A-12
What was foundFaceplate blank; handwritten 2A-12 on tape inside the cover
Where and whenRoom 2A, checked 2026-06-18, initials against the entry

Everything goes on that one sheet rather than into notes in the margin of the as-built. A check list can be counted, worked through and closed out; notes scattered across a drawing cannot be, and some will be missed.

Start the check list on the first walk and keep adding to it. It grows again at the closing check, the last pass before the records go, and it goes across with the records afterwards, so that every difference you found has either been corrected or been handed to somebody who can decide about it.

Correcting a label, and stopping short of that

Where the port map row and the hardware agree and the label is the only thing wrong — a blank faceplate on a position you have just confirmed, a label that has come unstuck — bringing the label into line is a label job. Do it, and note that you did.

Anything that would move a cord or change a port is not part of a labeling sweep. Record the difference and take it back for a decision before anything is touched.

A sweep compares two records, not the building

The labels and the rows can agree with each other and both be wrong, which happens whenever both were copied from the same as-built rather than from the hardware.

A sweep cannot settle that. The closing check reaches part of it, because one of its readings comes from the switch rather than from the paperwork, and tracing the run settles the rest.

529.6.6 Building the records with an AI assistant

Field notes come off a job as short lines written standing up. Turning them into the columns a site uses is the part an Artificial Intelligence (AI) chat tool does quickly, and it is also where a wrong value gets in.

The work is a conversion. You have notes taken across a day, in your own shorthand, in the order you walked the room. The site wants port map rows in a fixed column order, one row for each position, worded the same way throughout. An AI assistant will do that conversion from the notes and a list of the column names.

Why people reach for it

The reasons are good ones.

  • It is quick. A day of notes becomes a formatted table in under a minute.
  • It is on the phone in your pocket, at the end of a job, which is when the paperwork gets written and when you least want to write it.
  • It is consistent. Forty location entries come back worded the same way, instead of forty different ways.
  • What comes back looks finished. Even columns, no blanks, everything filled.

That last one is the problem. Left to itself, a tool does not mark the difference between a value it read in your notes and one it made up — both come back looking the same, and the second is invisible unless you go looking for it.

What an AI assistant does on this task, and what it cannot do.
It does this wellIt cannot do this
Puts your notes into the column order the site uses, and words every row the same wayKnow anything about the building that you did not put in the notes
Tells you which rows have a value missing, when you ask it toRead a value off the hardware. Every figure it returns came from your notes or from nowhere
Rewrites a location description into the same form for every deviceTell whether two notes about the same position are one row or two
Turns a rough note about a difference into the four parts of an exception lineTell whether a reading makes sense for the equipment in that room
Puts dates, units and identifiers into one format across every rowNotice that a whole position is missing from your notes, because it has no way to know what the room holds
Drafts the covering note once you tell it what was installed, what is outstanding and who to callTake responsibility for any of it. The name on the record is yours
The rule for every value

Every figure in the finished record traces back to a reading you took or a label you read. If you cannot find a value in your notes, delete it — and to fill that cell, somebody has to go and read it off the hardware.

Ask before you paste site data anywhere

Port maps, floor plans, device identifiers and IP addressing are restricted on many sites, and an AI chat tool sits outside the site.

Four things decide whether you may use one at all, and none of them is your judgement on the day.

  • The record owner. Whoever owns the port map decides what may leave it. Ask them, not your crew.
  • The contract. Some sites forbid customer data leaving their systems, and say so in writing before anybody arrives.
  • Your employer’s approved tools. Where a company account exists, that is the one to use. A personal account is not covered by it.
  • Names. An occupant’s name in a Serves column is personal data and carries rules of its own.

Get the answer before the job rather than while you are standing in the telecommunications room. Where the answer is no, the conversion is done by hand, and the notes are what make that possible.

How to ask

Three things go into the request, and the third is the one people leave out.

  • The column names, in the site’s order. Give them as a list. Without them you will get whatever columns the tool considers sensible, and they will not match the file you have to paste into.
  • The notes, unedited. Tidying them first smooths over the gaps and the ambiguities you want reported back to you.
  • Tell it to leave the empty cells empty. Ask it to put nothing in a cell it has no value for, and to list every cell it left blank and every note it could not place. Without that instruction it will fill the blanks with values of its own.

The same request works on the as-built. Give it your location notes and the four parts a location entry carries, and it returns every device in one form.

Two things that go wrong

Open both cards. One damages the record, the other damages the site.

What to check when it comes back

Read the returned port map against your notes one row at a time. Three faults account for most of what goes wrong.

  • A value that was not in the notes. A length, a class, a wattage or a date in a port map row that looks right and appears nowhere in what you wrote.
  • Two notes merged into one row. Where you wrote about the same position twice — once on the first visit and once after a repair — the two can arrive as a single row holding a mix of both.
  • A location description that reads well and names a different place. Shorthand gets expanded, and the expansion is a guess. Check every location entry against the note it came from.

What a day of notes does not contain

Below is one technician’s notes from one panel. The site’s port map has eight columns: Outlet, Panel / port, Switch port, VLAN, Serves, State, Last changed, Test ref. Read the notes against that list before you read what follows them.

Field notes — TR-2, panel B, Tuesday
b/11 -> gi1/0/11   2a-11   desk 2a      wire map ok, 41 m
b/12 -> gi1/0/24   2a-12   AP corridor 2   ok 63 m   vlan 20  cls3
b/14 -> gi1/0/12   2a-14   AP rm 2A tile C2   wire map ok 58 m   vlan 20 cls3 15.4 oper on
b/28  term, not patched   2b-04   tested ok
b/29  dead run, do not use
b/14 retest 14:20 after cord swap — 4/4 ping

Those six lines do not fill the eight columns. No row has a state, a last-changed date or a test reference, though two of the lines describe a state in words the column does not use. B / 29 has neither an outlet identifier, nor a reading, nor anything saying what it serves. The two B / 14 lines may be one row or they may be two. The b/11 line carries no VLAN, where the two lines around it do — and a VLAN number is the easiest of all of these to supply plausibly and the hardest to notice. And cls3 — the device’s power class — along with 15.4 and oper on belongs to the test record rather than to any of these columns, while tile C2 belongs on the as-built.

A tool given those notes and those eight column names will return eight full columns. Everything listed above is what it had to invent to do so.

529.6.7 Reading the records back against the install

The sweep held the labels against the port map rows — and a label is written from the record, so the two can agree while both are wrong about the room. The last check brings the install itself into the comparison.

Read the record first and say what you expect to find. Then look. Reading the hardware first and then turning to the record tends to confirm whatever the record says, because you already know the answer you are checking against.

What the closing check covers

The closing check is the last pass, run with the records in hand and the room still available to you. One part of it reads the switch and needs no walking; the rest holds the three records against each other and against the positions themselves, so keep the room until it is done. What it finds goes on the check list like any other exception.

  • Every row in the port map names a position you can find, carrying the label the row gives.
  • Every device drawn on the as-built has an identifier beside it, and that identifier has a row.
  • Every line in the test record names a position that exists.
  • Every position you worked on has a line in the test record. This is the one that catches a drop nobody ever tested.
  • Every switch port named in a row reads what the row says it should — the Status and Vlan columns of show interfaces status.
  • No two rows name the same switch port.
  • Anything in service appears in the port map and on the as-built.

Reading the switch ports back against the port map rows is the one check here you can make without walking to the room. Where you cannot reach the switch yourself, ask whoever owns it for the output, and record on the check list that you did.

Note what this does not reach. It tests what is patched, what is live and what is written down. Proving that a horizontal cable lands where its row says means tracing it, and that is its own job with the drop out of service. Where a label and a row agree and you have reason to doubt both, say so on the check list rather than ticking it off.

show interfaces status — read against the rows for panel B
Switch# show interfaces status

Gi1/0/11                     connected    20         a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX

Gi1/0/15                     connected    20         a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/24                     connected    1          a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
  • Gi1/0/11 and Gi1/0/12 — both read connected, and the Vlan column matches their rows. Outlets 2A-11 and 2A-14 are ticked off on the check list. The a- in a-full a-1000 means the two ends negotiated those values rather than being fixed to them, and 10/100/1000BaseTX is what the port itself can run at over copper.
  • Gi1/0/15 — a working link on a port that no row in the full port map names. Something is patched and in service and written down nowhere. Ask whoever owns the switch what is on that port, put the answer on the check list, and hand it over as an exception if nobody can say.
  • Gi1/0/24 — the row for outlet 2A-12 gives Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) 20, and the Vlan column reads 1. One of the two is wrong. Which one gets corrected is a question for whoever owns the switch, and it is an exception line either way.

Port names differ between switches; where a switch names its ports Fa1/0/12 rather than Gi1/0/12, the columns read the same.

Checking part of a room, and saying so

A full check on a large room costs a day. Sites often accept a sample, and a sample holds up as long as the record says what it was.

  • How many port map rows were checked, as a count and out of how many. The same goes for the two walks: if you swept forty rows out of two hundred, the record says forty out of two hundred.
  • How they were chosen — every fourth row, or every row changed since the last check, or all the powered drops. Whichever it was, name it.
  • What the exceptions were, and how many rows they came from.

A record that says checked, with none of that behind it, does not tell the next reader whether some of the room was checked or all of it.

Everything this check turns up goes on the same check list the walks have been feeding. By the time you stop, that list is the honest part of the hand-off: it says what you found, what you put right, and what you could not.

The check is written down whether or not it finds anything

A closing check that raised no exceptions is recorded as a closing check that raised no exceptions, with the date, the scope and a name.

If nothing records the check, the next person has no way of knowing it happened.

Lesson 529.6 · Section 2 Proving the Records and Handing Them Over

529.6.8 The hand-off

A hand-off goes to a named person on a named day. Leaving a folder where somebody could find it is not one.

Five things cross over: the three records, the check list you have been adding to since the first walk, and a covering note.

The hand-off package.
ItemWhat it has to carry
Port mapA row for every position the panel terminates, spares and retired positions included, each row carrying its state and its date
As-builtOutlets, pathways and devices at their positions, each with its identifier beside it. The drawing carries a date and a revision mark
Test recordOne line per drop: what was measured, the value, the tool, the date, and who took the reading
Check listEach check with its date, what it covered and who made it; every row ticked off or carrying an exception line; and the state of each exception — corrected, reported, or still open
Covering noteWhat was installed, what was tested and to what, what is outstanding, and who to call

Outstanding items go in writing

Most jobs finish with something unfinished, and each one of those is a line in the covering note.

  • A position terminated and left unpatched because the device had not arrived.
  • A label ordered and not yet fitted, with a handwritten label in place meanwhile.
  • A run that the site wants certified, which needs a different tool and a separate visit.
  • A discrepancy reported and not yet decided on by whoever owns it.

An outstanding item that was named on the day is part of the job. The same item unwritten becomes a defect somebody else discovers.

Do not write a position you did not check as working

A port map row nobody verified is recorded as not checked. Fill one in from the work order, on the assumption that it ought to be right, and it reads the same as a row somebody read off the hardware.

That is the port map row that sends the next technician to the wrong place with confidence.

What accepted means

Name the person the set went to, and the date it went. Where the site has a form, the person receiving it signs; where it does not, an email listing what was sent does the same job.

Accepted means somebody has said they hold it. Until then the records are still yours, and so is anything found missing from them.

Read the covering note back as if you were the person receiving it. It should answer three questions: what state is this room in, what still needs doing, and who to call about it.

Lesson 529.6 · Section 3 Wrapping Up

529.6.9 What you covered

Write the three records, check the labels against them, check them against the room, and hand them to a named person on a named day.

A finished install leaves three records. The port map says which switch port serves an outlet and what is on the end of it. The as-built says where the outlets and the devices are in the building. The test record says what each drop measured, when, with which tool, and who took the reading. The port map describes the room as it is today. The as-built is not the design drawing, which recorded what was intended. A test result describes one day and does not update itself, so the date is read with it. One drop carries the same identifier in all three, and that identifier lets a reader move between them. ANSI/TIA-606-C covers identifiers, labels, records and drawings as one subject: it sets out how an identifier is built and leaves the values to the site, and it requires the site to have a written scheme and to keep the labels and the records in step. Find the scheme before you read a label or write one. Find out too where all three records live and which of them you are allowed to change, because a good record written into the wrong file leaves the site with two records that disagree.

The port map belongs to the room, not to the job, so it carries a row for every termination position and not only the ones you worked on. That includes the spares — a position with no row is invisible to whoever is asked to bring up a new desk. Three columns only make sense across a whole room: a state column so a blank cell is never ambiguous, a last changed column, and a test reference so a reader can get from the row to the measurement. The state column runs in service, spare, reserved or retired. Three rules then hold the set of rows together. No two rows may name the same switch port, because a port serves one position at a time — where two rows do, one is stale and the map cannot say which. A position taken out of service keeps its row, with the state changed to retired and dated. And the old value is written down before the new one is typed — at the drop, into the port map or into your notes, rather than reconstructed from memory afterwards.

A room number gets somebody into the room; a device on a suspended ceiling can be at any of dozens of tiles. A location entry has four parts: the space, the position within the space measured from something that will still be there, the mount, and which way it faces for anything directional — written as not applicable where it is not, rather than left blank. On the drawing, a device gets a symbol at its position, with the identifier from its port map row printed beside it. The drawing carries a date and a revision mark. The device and the outlet it is patched to are two different positions in the building, and both are recorded.

Verified is not certified. A verifier reads how the cabling is wired and how long it is, and reports whether power is present and what speed the far end advertised. A certifier measures performance — insertion loss, crosstalk and the rest — and its report can say a run meets a named category, as a permanent link or as a channel. A test record built from a wire map and a length reading may say the run is wired correctly and within length on the day it was tested. It may not say the run meets a category, because nothing in it measured one. Where a site wants a category on the record, that is a request for a certification test.

The four readings for a powered drop are recorded by column and value, with the command named. Link comes from the Status, Duplex, Speed and Vlan columns of show interfaces status. Power comes from the Admin, Oper, Class and Power columns of show power inline, where Power is what the port has set aside rather than what the device is drawing. It is recorded as Oper on, class 3, 15.4 W set aside rather than as power fine. Presence on the port is whether show mac address-table interface lists a Media Access Control (MAC) address for that port. The first three come off the switch. Reachability is a ping you run from a machine in the same Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN), recorded as counts sent and received.

A labeling sweep checks one row against four labels in two places: the faceplate, both ends of the horizontal cable, and the panel position. Two of the four sit behind something. The switch port carries no label at all and is read from the switch. The sweep runs in two directions, and running it one way leaves the other kind of defect standing. Record to hardware finds hardware that is missing, unlabeled or wrong. Hardware to record finds a label with no row — an outlet added in a later fit-out, a panel position labeled and never written down, a device somebody put in on a Friday. A row that agrees is ticked off on the check list rather than dated in the map; a row that does not gets an exception line naming the position, what the record says, what was found, and where and when. All of it goes on the check list, started on the first walk and added to at the closing check: the date, scope and name of each check, every row ticked off, and every exception line. The check list crosses over with the other records at the end. Bringing a label into line, where the row and the hardware agree and only the label is wrong, is a label job; anything that would move a cord or change a port goes back for a decision. A sweep compares two records against each other, so labels and rows copied from the same wrong drawing will agree with one another.

An Artificial Intelligence (AI) chat tool converts field notes into the site’s columns quickly and consistently, and it knows nothing about the building beyond what the notes contain. Every figure it returns came from your notes or from nowhere. Every figure in the finished record traces back to a reading you took or a label you read. Three things go into the request: the column names in the site’s order, the notes unedited, and an instruction to leave the empty cells empty and list them. Check the result row by row for a value that was not in the notes, two notes merged into one row, and a location description that reads well and names a different place. Port maps, floor plans, device identifiers and Internet Protocol (IP) addressing are restricted on many sites, so find out whether they may be pasted into an outside tool before the job.

The closing check runs from the record out to the install: read the row, say what you expect, then look. Seven things are checked. Every row names a position you can find, carrying the label the row gives. Every device on the as-built has an identifier, and that identifier has a port map row. Every test line names a position that exists, and every position you worked on has a test line — the direction that catches a drop nobody ever tested. Every switch port named in a row reads what the row says, in the Status and Vlan columns of show interfaces status. No two rows name one port. Anything in service appears in the port map and on the as-built — and a working link on a port that no row names is only visible from the switch. Where the check is a sample, the record says how many rows out of how many, how they were chosen, what the exceptions were, and how many rows they came from. A check that found nothing is written down on the check list as a check that found nothing, with the date, the scope and a name. What none of it reaches is whether a horizontal cable lands where its row says; that needs a trace, with the drop out of service.

The hand-off is to a person, on a date

Five things cross over: the port map, the as-built, the test record, the check list, and a covering note. The check list carries every check made and the state of each exception on it — corrected, reported or still open. The covering note says what was installed, what was tested and to what, what is outstanding, and who to call. Outstanding items named on the day are part of the job; the same items unwritten become defects found by somebody else. A position nobody checked is recorded as not checked, never as working. Accepted means a named person has said they hold the set. Where the site has a form, the person receiving it signs; where it does not, an email listing what was sent does the same job. Until then the records are still yours.
