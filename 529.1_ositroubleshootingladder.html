529.1.1 “It does not work” is not a diagnosis

Lesson 529.1

OSI as a Troubleshooting Ladder

The Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model is usually taught as seven names to memorize. Used as a list of names it does nothing for you. Used as an order of work it tells you what to check first, what to leave for now, and where your responsibility ends.

  • Map cable, connectors and patch panels to Layer 1.
  • Map switches, Media Access Control (MAC) addressing and Power over Ethernet (PoE) to Layers 1 and 2.
  • Trace data flow from an end device to the switch.
  • Explain where a cabling fault appears in the model.
  • Relate troubleshooting order to the layer stack.

The most common sentence a technician hears is also the least useful one.

Someone tells you the network is down. Consider what that sentence actually contains. It does not tell you whether a cable is damaged, whether a switch port is disabled, whether a laptop has the wrong Internet Protocol (IP) address, whether a server two states away has failed, or whether somebody mistyped a password. It reports a symptom, and the symptom is identical for all of those causes.

Without a method, what fills the gap is guessing. Guessing is not free. It has four costs that show up on real job sites:

  • You replace a cable that was working, and the fault is still there.
  • You reboot a switch that twenty other people were using.
  • You escalate something that was yours to fix, and lose credibility.
  • You spend an hour on something that was never yours to begin with.

The OSI model gives you something better than a guess. It describes moving data as seven numbered layers, each one a group of functions that the layers above it rely on. Nothing in a network is literally divided this way — the model is a shared way of describing the job, not a diagram of the equipment.

The model is several things at once, and it is worth being clear about all of them:

  • A way of managing complexity. Delivering data across a network is a large job. The model splits it into smaller, separate functions, so each one can be designed, built and repaired without having to understand the whole.
  • A framework that standards are written against. This is why a Cisco switch, a Ubiquiti access point and somebody’s laptop interoperate without ever having been designed together.
  • A common language. “This is a Layer 1 problem” means the same thing to you, to the network team, and to a manufacturer’s support line.
  • A troubleshooting tool, which is the use you will get most from and the one the rest of this lesson develops.
Everything can check out correct and the service can still fail

What fails is not “a layer”. What fails is the service — data not reaching its destination — and that can originate at any layer, or at more than one at once.

Here is the part that catches people. Suppose a device is patched into a switch port that has been switched off. Check the program: running normally. Check the firewall: the right ports are open. Check the IP address: correct. Those checks all return a clean result, and the service still does not work. Nothing above the fault looks wrong — that is exactly the difficulty. You can spend an afternoon confirming that things are fine and learn nothing, because you were never looking where the disruption originated.

This is one of the things the model is good for. Used this way it does not hand you the answer — it tells you where you have looked and where you have not.

Here is what that looks like on the job. A desk phone shows no link light. There is no signal reaching it. You could now spend twenty minutes checking its IP address, its subnet mask, and its gateway — the router it sends traffic through to reach anything outside its own network — and all of them could be correct. None can help, because no signal is arriving at the port for those settings to act on. The IP address is not wrong. It is irrelevant while nothing is reaching the device.

The model matters to you in a particular way, because you own the bottom of the stack. The cable, the jack, the patch panel, the port, the power delivered over that cable — that is your work, and it is where most outages actually begin.

529.1.2 OSI Model — the seven layers

Seven layers, numbered one to seven, counting up from the physical medium.

Layer 7 is the program a person is using. Layer 1 is the physical medium — copper, fiber or radio. When a device sends something the data travels down from Layer 7 to Layer 1, where it leaves as a signal. When a device receives something it travels up from Layer 1 to Layer 7. Layer 1 carries the number 1 because it is where data enters and leaves the physical world.

The cards below run from Layer 7 at the top to Layer 1 at the bottom, the same way the layers are drawn. Open each one.

Why the numbers get used more than the names

You will hear the names; you will use the numbers. Saying “this is a Layer 1 problem” is quicker and less ambiguous than describing a symptom, and it is the language the network team uses as well.

Place each of these at the layer it belongs to.

529.1.3 Wrapping and unwrapping: data in transit

The layers are not filing cabinets. They describe something that physically happens to data on its way out of a computer.

So far the model has been a way of sorting responsibilities. That is useful, but it is not what the model is. The seven layers describe a process: as data leaves a device it travels down the stack, and the lower layers each add something to it. When it arrives somewhere it travels up the stack, and each of those additions is removed in turn. Adding the wrappings on the way down is called encapsulation. Removing them again on the way up is de-encapsulation.

Think of posting a document. You write the page, put it in an envelope, address the envelope, then hand it to a courier who puts it in a sack with hundreds of others. Two separate wrappings, each with its own addressing, each needed by a different handler. Nobody along the route reads your document — they read the wrapping that concerns them and pass the rest along. That is encapsulation: your content is unchanged, but each handler that needs its own addressing adds a wrapping around it.

Now follow it to the other end. The sack arrives and is opened, and your envelope is taken out. The envelope is opened, and your page is taken out. Each handler removes only the wrapping that was added for them, in the reverse order it was added, until the page reaches the person it was addressed to — who reads it, and never sees the sack at all. That is de-encapsulation.

Every device on a network does both, constantly: encapsulating what it sends, de-encapsulating what it receives.

Data does the same. On the way down, Layer 4, then Layer 3, then Layer 2 each add a small block of information at the front — a header — carrying the addressing that layer needs, then hands the whole bundle down to the layer below. Layer 2 adds a block at the end as well, a check value the receiving switch uses to test whether the frame arrived intact.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram has five rows, read downward, showing what happens to data as it is sent.

  1. Row 1. A single wide block labeled Data. This is your message, as it exists at Layers 7 to 5.
  2. Row 2. A Layer 4 header block is added to the left of the Data block. The bundle is now called a segment, and the added header carries port numbers.
  3. Row 3. A Layer 3 header is added to the left of that. The bundle is now called a packet, and the added header carries IP addresses.
  4. Row 4. A Layer 2 header is added to the left of that. The bundle is now called a frame, and the added header carries MAC addresses.
  5. Row 5. No blocks at all, only a wave. Layer 1 has converted the frame into a signal on the physical medium — voltage on copper, light on fiber, or radio over the air.

An arrow down the left is labeled Sending, for encapsulation. An arrow up the right is labeled Receiving, for de-encapsulation, where each header is removed in the reverse order it was added.

Two things in that diagram matter more than the vocabulary. The first is that your data is never opened along the way. A switch reads the Layer 2 header, decides which port to send the frame out of, and does not look inside. It has no idea whether it is carrying an email or a phone call. That is exactly why one switch can carry every kind of traffic without being told what any of it is.

The second is what this means for the cable you install. What crosses your cable is not a message. It is a signal — voltage on copper, pulses of light on fiber, radio between an access point and a laptop. Layer 1 makes one promise only: that the bits which went in come out the other end unchanged. It promises nothing about MAC or IP addresses, nothing about switch settings, and nothing about whether the application works.

Why a passing cable test proves less than people think

A wire map test confirms that the pairs are in the right order and that signal survives the run. That is Layer 1, and only Layer 1. It is necessary and it is not sufficient — a perfect cable still reaches nothing if the port is switched off or the IP address is wrong. Knowing exactly what your test proved, and what it did not, is what stops you saying “the cable is fine” when you mean “the cable is not the problem, but something else is.”

The same chain runs the other way. Damage the cable and the frame arrives corrupted. A corrupted frame is discarded, so the packet inside it never arrives, so the application never responds — and the user reports that the internet is broken. One physical fault, reported as a Layer 7 symptom. That is the pattern you will spend the rest of this lesson learning to unwind — starting with which of those layers is actually yours.

529.1.4 The layers that are yours

Seven layers exist. Two are yours, and one you read.

With the layers named and the movement of data understood, the model can do another job: showing which problems belong to a low-voltage technician.

Where a low-voltage technician’s responsibility sits against the seven layers.
LayersYour responsibilityWhat that means in practice
1 and 2YoursYou install it, test it, configure the port, and fix it
3Yours to observeYou read the addressing and report it accurately; changing it is usually somebody else’s call
4 to 7Network teamYou escalate, with evidence of what you already checked

Layer 1 is the cable or fiber you pulled, the jack you punched down, the panel it lands on, the port it plugs into, and the power that port supplies. Layer 2 is the switch: which port a device is on, and whether that port is passing traffic. Those two layers are your trade.

Layer 3 sits on the boundary. You will read IP addresses constantly and you need to report them precisely, but deciding what they should be is generally not your call.

Low layer does not mean low importance

It is easy to read a stack diagram as a ranking, with the interesting work at the top. Resist that. Layer 1 problems — a damaged cable, a loose jack, a port with no power — are among the most common causes of real outages, and they are almost always the fastest to fix once found. The layer you own is the layer that fails most.

One sentence to carry out of this topic: knowing which layer a fault sits in tells you whether to pick up a tool or pick up the phone. The next topic sets out the three ways of working through the layers, and later in the lesson that becomes four questions you can ask in order.

529.1.5 Three ways to work through the layers

Bottom to top is not the only route. There are three, and each suits a different situation.

Now that the layers have names and numbers, the model can be used as a search method. Which direction you travel depends on what you already know when the problem reaches you.

Three approaches to troubleshooting with the OSI model.
ApproachHow it runsWhen to reach for it
Bottom–upStart at Layer 1 and work upwardWhen physical work has just been done, or a physical fault is likely. The technician’s usual default
Top–downStart at Layer 7 and work downwardWhen the complaint is about one program and everything else on the same device works
Divide and conquerStart in the middle, usually Layer 3 with a ping. If it fails, work down. If it succeeds, work upWhen there is no strong clue where to begin. One test halves the possibilities

Divide and conquer is often the quickest, because a single ping reports on the lower half of the stack in one step. If the ping succeeds, Layers 1 through 3 are delivering data and the search moves upward.

Bottom–up is the low-voltage technician’s usual starting point, for a practical reason rather than a theoretical one: the layers you own are at the bottom, and a fault there is both common and quick to confirm.

The approach is a starting point, not a rule

None of the three is more correct than the others. They are ways of ordering a search, and a technician who already has good evidence may reasonably begin anywhere. Whichever you use, it has to leave you able to say which layers you have eliminated and by what reading.

529.1.6 Layer 1: what you install, and what it promises

Layer 1 makes one promise: the bits that went in come out the other end unchanged. Everything else is somebody else’s layer.

Layer 1 has no idea what it is carrying. It holds no MAC or IP addresses, keeps no record of what it sent, and cannot tell a video call from a print job. It converts bits into a signal, moves that signal along a medium, and converts it back. That is the whole job.

The parts you install are all Layer 1: the cable or fiber, the connectors on each end, the keystone jack, the patch panel, the patch cords, and the port each end plugs into. The electronics that turns bits into a signal is Layer 1 too — the network interface in a laptop, and the matching circuitry on a switch port.

What Layer 1 does and does not take responsibility for.
Layer 1 is responsible forLayer 1 is not responsible for
Turning bits into a signal and backKnowing which device the data is for
Carrying that signal the required distanceDeciding which port to send it out of
Delivering the bit pattern unchangedNoticing or repairing a corrupted bit pattern
Supplying a link at an agreed speedAnything about MAC or IP addresses, programs or users

Two consequences follow, and both show up in real fault finding.

Distance is a Layer 1 constraint. A signal weakens as it travels. The structured cabling standard is ANSI/TIA-568, from the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the Telecommunications Industry Association (TIA). It sets the maximum length of a horizontal copper channel at 100 meters — 90 meters of permanent run, and the rest patch cords. Exceed it and the link may still appear to work while quietly losing bits.

“Working” describes more than one state. A link can be perfect, dead, or marginal. A marginal link passes most bits and corrupts some — often when the cable warms up, or when a nearby motor starts. The symptom is a connection that drops for a second and returns, which is far harder to find than a link that does not come up. IEEE 802.3, the Ethernet standard from the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), says what the equipment at each end must cope with. How much signal loss and interference the cabling itself may carry is set by ANSI/TIA-568, and that is what a certification tester measures a run against.

Why intermittent faults are worth naming early

A link that fails outright announces itself. A marginal one does not, and the complaint that reaches you will describe the program, not the cable. If you have already met the idea that Layer 1 can be partly working, an intermittent report becomes a clue rather than a mystery.

529.1.7 Copper, fiber and radio on the model

Three media, one layer, one promise — and three different sets of things that go wrong.

All three media sit at Layer 1 and all three make the same promise. What differs is the physics, and the physics decides how each one fails. Knowing the failure modes of the medium in front of you is most of what makes a Layer 1 diagnosis quick.

The three physical media, and how each tends to fail.
MediumCarries Typical failureWhat it is sensitive to
CopperVoltage on twisted pairsBroken conductor, wrong pair order, corrosionElectrical interference, distance, tight bends, untwisting the pairs
FiberPulses of lightDirty or damaged end face, a break in the glassContamination, bend radius, physical shock
RadioRadio waves through the airWeak signal, congestion, interferenceDistance, walls, other radio sources, number of devices sharing it

One difference is worth drawing out. Copper carries an electrical signal, so an electrical field nearby can induce noise into it — a motor, a fluorescent ballast, or a power cable run alongside it. Fiber carries light and no electrical current, so none of that reaches it. When a copper run has to pass near heavy electrical equipment, that is a reason to consider fiber rather than a preference.

Radio differs in another way: it is shared. A copper run belongs to one link, but every device within range of an access point uses the same air. A radio link can therefore degrade because more people arrived, with nothing physically wrong at all.

The same layer, a different first question

On copper, the first question is usually about the terminations and what runs alongside the cable. On fiber, it is almost always about cleanliness at the end faces. On radio, it is about distance, obstructions and how many devices are competing. Same layer, same promise, three different opening moves.

Lesson 529.1 · Section 2 The Layers You Own

529.1.8 Layer 2: what the switch decides

Layer 1 moves the signal. Layer 2 decides where it goes next.

A switch has one central job. A frame arrives on one port, and the switch decides which port or ports it should leave by. To make that decision it reads the frame’s Layer 2 header, which carries the MAC address of the device the frame is for. A MAC address is the Layer 2 address, built into every network interface, and the next topic covers where it comes from. The switch does not open the packet inside, and it does not read the IP address — that is a Layer 3 address and it belongs to a router.

There are three possible decisions, and each produces a different symptom.

A VLAN constrains all of this. Assigning ports to different VLANs tells the switch that those groups of ports may not exchange frames, and that a broadcast from one group must not reach the other. One physical switch then behaves as though it were several separate switches.

Why the discard case matters to you

Of the three decisions, the third is the one that will bring you a ticket. Corrupted frames are discarded without any message to the user, so the report you receive describes a program that will not load. The cause is at Layer 1, the evidence is at Layer 2, and the complaint arrives from Layer 7.

529.1.9 MAC addressing: how a switch knows what is where

Nobody tells a switch which device is on which port. It works it out by watching.

Every network interface is manufactured with a MAC address: six pairs of characters, such as 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E. The first half identifies the manufacturer, the second half is unique to that individual interface. Nobody on site assigns it, and it does not normally change — which is the opposite of an IP address, and the reason the two are diagnosed differently.

A switch builds its own record of where devices are, called the MAC address table. It fills that table by observation: every time a frame arrives, the switch notes the sender’s address and the port it arrived on. Nothing is configured. The switch remembers who it has heard from, and where.

Text description of this diagram

On the left, a box shows a frame arriving on port 12, with a sender MAC address of 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E. An arrow labeled “note it” points right to a second box titled MAC address table.

The table has two columns, Address and Port, and two rows. The first row pairs 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E with port 12. The second row pairs 00:4F:6E:1C:8A:22 with port 7.

Text beneath states that the next time a frame is addressed to 00:1A:2B:3C:4D:5E, the switch sends it out of port 12 only, and that a MAC address the table has never seen is sent to every port in the VLAN instead.

This is the mechanism behind the two switch decisions from the previous topic. A destination in the table gets forwarded to one port. A destination that is not in the table gets sent everywhere, because the switch has no better option than to ask.

Entries do not last forever. If a switch hears nothing from a MAC address for a period, it removes the entry, so that a device which has been moved to a different port is not sent traffic at the old one.

What the MAC address table answers

Asking a switch to show its MAC address table for a port answers a specific question: has this switch heard anything at all from the device on that port? An entry proves the device is powered, connected, and sending frames that arrive intact. An empty result sends you back to the cable, the connector, the device, or the port’s own power setting — a device the switch has not powered has nothing to send.

529.1.10 Power over Ethernet sits at Layer 1, and the switch decides it

One cable, two jobs. The data was always the point; the power came later, and it is the switch that decides whether to supply it.

Power over Ethernet delivers electrical power to a device along the same cable that carries its data. It is what allows a camera, an access point or a desk phone to be installed with one cable and no power outlet nearby, which is why so much low-voltage work now depends on it.

The power itself is Layer 1 — electricity on the same conductors, alongside the signal. What makes it different from the rest of Layer 1 is that it is switched on and off by a setting. Power is not sitting on the cable waiting: the switch tests the device first, works out how much power it is asking for, and then supplies it. Each port is decided separately, and a port can be told not to supply power at all.

The sequence a switch follows before supplying power to a port.
StepWhat happensIf it does not happen
1. DetectionThe switch applies a small voltage to check whether a device designed for PoE is presentNo power is supplied, which protects equipment that is not built for it
2. ClassificationThe device declares a power class, telling the switch roughly how much it needsThe switch may allocate a default amount, or refuse
3. SupplyThe switch delivers power, provided its remaining budget allowsThe port stays unpowered even though the device is present and correct

Two standards govern this, both part of IEEE 802.3. The earlier one, 802.3af, supplies up to about 15 watts per port. The later 802.3at, often called PoE+, supplies up to about 30 watts. A switch built to the earlier standard cannot power a device that requires the later one, no matter how the port is configured.

There is also a limit on the switch as a whole. Every switch has a total power budget shared across all its ports, so a switch can be perfectly capable of powering a device and still refuse, because earlier ports have already claimed the available power.

Three separate reasons a PoE device stays dark

The port is configured not to supply power. The device needs more power than this switch can provide per port. Or the switch has run out of overall budget. All three look identical from the desk — a device that does nothing — and none of the three is a cabling fault. The cable can be perfect in every case, which is why the reading is taken at the switch rather than with a tester.

529.1.11 Tracing data from a desk device to a switch port

Between a laptop on a desk and a port on a switch there are seven physical elements. Any one of them can be the fault.

Every element from the device to the switch port is Layer 1, the port included. Layer 2 is not a different piece of hardware: it is what the switch does with the frames that arrive at that port, and it is the first point on the path where anything decides where traffic goes. Knowing the elements in order matters, because a fault report never tells you which one is at issue.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram follows a single path, left to right along the top and then back right to left along the bottom.

  1. Device interface
  2. Patch cord
  3. Wall jack
  4. Horizontal run
  5. Patch panel
  6. Patch cord, on the lower row
  7. Switch port, highlighted, the last Layer 1 element and where the switch starts deciding

A heading states that the path is Layer 1 all the way until the switch port. A note records that there are twelve ends in total: the five middle elements have two ends each, while the device interface and the switch port have one each.

Count the ends rather than the elements. The five in the middle each have two — the two patch cords, the jack, the run and the panel — while the device interface and the switch port have one each. That is twelve ends, and any one of them can be loose, dirty, miswired or broken. When somebody says “it must be the cable,” they are usually describing one of those twelve rather than the cable itself.

The horizontal run is the most difficult element to inspect, since most of it is inside a wall or a pathway. That is also the one you are least likely to have caused, because it was terminated once and then left alone. The patch cords at each end are handled constantly and get moved, pinched and swapped, which is why they are worth suspecting early.

Put the path in order, from the device to the switch.

The network interface in the device itself.
The patch cord from the device to the wall jack.
The keystone jack in the faceplate at the desk.
The horizontal run through the wall or pathway.
The punch-down termination at the patch panel.
The patch cord from the patch panel to the switch.
The switch port, the last Layer 1 element.

529.1.12 Where a cabling fault appears in the model

A cabling fault originates at Layer 1, but it produces a different observable symptom at every layer above it.

This is the point where the model earns its keep. A single physical fault does not produce one symptom — it produces a different symptom at every layer above it. If you know what a Layer 1 fault looks like from Layer 2, or from Layer 3, you can recognize it without having to go and inspect the cable first.

One broken conductor, seen from four different layers.
Looking atWhat you observeWhat it tempts you to conclude
Layer 1No link light at either endCorrect as far as it goes — though a port shut down, or set never to supply power, looks the same from the desk
Layer 2No MAC address table entry for the portWrongly, that the switch is misconfigured
Layer 3Ping fails, no address obtainedWrongly, that addressing is the problem
Layer 7The program will not open or times outWrongly, that the program or server is at fault

Now consider a different fault: a split pair, where two conductors are terminated into the wrong pairs. Continuity is intact, so a test that only checks continuity reports the run as good, and the link often comes up. What fails is reliability at higher speeds. The observations change:

A split pair, seen from the same four layers.
Looking atWhat you observe
Layer 1Link light present. Continuity is intact end to end, so a continuity check passes it. A wire map tester finds it by checking the pairing itself
Layer 2A MAC entry appears, then frames are discarded as damaged
Layer 3Ping works sometimes and fails sometimes
Layer 7The program is slow, or drops out and recovers
The pattern behind the symptoms

A fault that stops everything is usually a broken connection. A fault that makes things unreliable rather than dead is usually a marginal or miswired one. Both originate at Layer 1, and neither can be identified from a Layer 7 complaint.

Lesson 529.1 · Section 3 Finding the Fault

529.1.13 Why one physical fault becomes a complaint about a program

The person reporting a fault can only see Layer 7. That is the only layer they have.

Follow a single damaged conductor upward through the stack and watch what happens to the symptom at each step.

How one physical fault travels up the layers to become a user complaint.
LayerWhat is actually happening
1A conductor is damaged, so the signal arriving is distorted
2Frames arrive corrupted and fail their check, so the switch discards them
3The packets inside those frames never arrive, so nothing is reachable
4Delivery is never confirmed, so the sender keeps retrying and then gives up
7The program waits, shows an error, and the person says the system is down

Nobody in that chain is wrong. The program did fail. Delivery was never confirmed. Packets did not arrive. Frames were discarded. Every layer reported accurately on what it could see, and the only person who spoke was the one at the top.

Two things follow from this, and they are the practical value of this whole lesson.

A fault report tells you the symptom, not the location. “The system is down” is compatible with a damaged cable, a disabled port, a wrong IP address, a failed server, and an expired password. Treating the report as a description of the cause is what leads to replacing working equipment.

Translating the report is part of your job. The person is describing Layer 7 because that is what they can see. Your task is to convert that into a question you can answer with a tool — and the layers you own are at the bottom, which is where the next topic starts.

Take the report seriously, and take it literally

A report that sounds vague is not a careless one. “It was fine yesterday” and “only when I move my laptop” are Layer 1 clues stated in the only vocabulary available to the person giving them. Ask what changed and when, rather than dismissing the description.

529.1.14 The four questions, in order

Seven layers, reduced to four questions you can ask standing at a desk.

Working bottom–up, the model collapses into a short sequence. Each question has a tool that answers it and a piece of evidence you can record. Ask them in order and stop at the first one that answers no, because that is where the search ends and the work begins.

Text description of this diagram

Four stacked bars, read from the bottom upward, each labeled with a question and who owns it.

  1. Bottom bar: is it connected, and is the medium good? Marked Yours.
  2. Second bar: is the port up, in the right VLAN, and powered? Marked Yours.
  3. Third bar: does it have an IP address, and can it reach? Marked Observe.
  4. Top bar, highlighted: anything above that. Marked Escalate.

A label down the right side reads “ask in this order”.

The order is not arbitrary. Each question depends on the ones below it having been answered yes, so answering them upward means you never spend time on a setting that could not have worked anyway.

Stop at the first no

A no at step 1 is what you work on next, not something to climb past. The VLAN and the addressing above it cannot be read while nothing is arriving, and asking anyway produces readings that look like evidence and are not.

Power is the one part of step 2 that belongs to resolving step 1. A device that takes all its power from the port cannot bring a link up until the port supplies it, so on a powered device, reading whether the port is supplying power is part of answering step 1 rather than a step beyond it.

Lesson 529.1 · Section 3 Finding the Fault

529.1.15 Whose problem is it, and where your scope ends

Knowing where your scope ends is part of the trade, and saying so clearly is a skill.

Steps 1 and 2 of the ladder are yours to resolve. Step 3 you read and report. Step 4 belongs to somebody else. Reaching step 4 is not a failure to solve the problem — it is the correct outcome, provided you hand it over in a usable state.

What makes a hand-over usable is evidence. Compare these two:

Two versions of the same escalation.
ThinUsable
“Cable is fine, must be a network problem.”“Run tested good on a wire map. Port 12 is up at 1 Gbps, in VLAN 20, drawing 6 W. Device has an IP address and pings its gateway. It cannot reach anything beyond that. Handing over at Layer 3 and above.”

The second version does three things the first does not. It says what was tested and how. It gives the values, so nobody has to repeat the work. And it names where the boundary was drawn, so the next person knows exactly what remains unexamined.

It also protects you. A hand-over that records what you verified is the difference between a fault that moves forward and a fault that comes back with your name on it.

Three things a usable hand-over contains
  • What you verified — the steps you completed and the tool you used.
  • What you found — the actual values, not a judgement about them.
  • Where you stopped — the layer you are handing over at, and why.

Notice that none of this requires knowing what is wrong at the layer above yours.

Lesson 529.1 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

529.1.16 What you covered

One model, four questions, and a clear line around what is yours.

The OSI model describes moving data as seven layers of function. It is a way of managing a large job by splitting it up, a framework that standards are written against, a shared language for describing problems, and a method for searching when something does not work.

Summary of the layers, and where a low-voltage technician’s work sits.
LayersWhat happens thereYours?
4 to 7Delivery confirmation, sessions, formatting, and the program itselfNo — escalate
3IP addressing, subnets, routing between networksRead and report
2Switches, MAC addressing, VLANsYes
1Copper, fiber and radio, connectors, jacks, panels, ports, Power over EthernetYes

Data is wrapped on the way down — encapsulation — and unwrapped on the way up, as de-encapsulation. Each device reads only the wrapping meant for it, which is why a switch forwards traffic without knowing what it carries.

There are three ways to search: bottom–up, top–down, and divide and conquer starting with a ping. Bottom–up is the usual starting point for a low-voltage technician, because the layers you own are at the bottom.

The four questions, once more
  1. Is it connected, and is the medium good?
  2. Is the port up, in the right VLAN, and supplying power?
  3. Does it have an IP address, and can it reach anything?
  4. Anything above that — escalate, with evidence.

Ask them in order. Stop at the first no.

The last idea is the one that carries furthest. A fault report describes a symptom, not a location, because the person reporting it can only see the top of the stack. Turning that report into a question you can answer with a tool is the work — and recording what you checked is what makes the answer worth anything to whoever comes next.
