Lesson 529.4 — PoE Device Connection and Verification

The written half of this lesson.

This lesson prepares you for connecting a powered device to a Power over Ethernet (PoE) switch port and proving it works.

You read the device’s specification sheet to find out whether the port can power it. You size a set of devices against the power the switch has to share. You patch the device through the run, then take four readings that say what the drop is doing.

Those four readings also tell you which stage failed when one comes back bad — powering, linking, or reaching the network — and whether the fix is yours or somebody else’s.

How the lesson is organized

  • Sizing the Device to the Port. A powered device draws a certain number of watts. The switch port sets aside a figure of its own — normally the larger of the two, taken from the PoE class the device reports, and trimmed where the device negotiates for less. This section covers where to find both numbers, and how to work out how many devices one switch can power.
  • Connecting and Proving It. You patch the device through the run without disturbing the cords already in the rack. The switch then tells you whether power is flowing, and at what speed and duplex the link came up. A ping from the same Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) shows whether anything can reach the device.
  • Closing the Ticket. The port map row says which outlet is patched to which switch port. You add the four readings to it, plus the date and your name. Read together, those readings also narrow a fault to one place.
  • Wrapping Up. The whole lesson in one place.

A note on the equipment

Switches differ in two ways that affect what you will see on the equipment in front of you. Port names differ, so a command shown here may take a different form on your switch. They also do not all supply the same amount of power: a port built to the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 802.3af standard supplies up to 15.4 W, and one built to 802.3at supplies up to 30.0 W. Both the per-port ceiling and the shared pool are read from the switch in front of you rather than carried over from the last one.

529.4.1 What the device draws, and what the port sets aside

Lesson 529.4

PoE Device Connection and Verification

A configured port is not a working drop. This lesson takes you from a device in a box to a device that is powered, linked at the speed the run supports, reachable by something else on its network, and written into the record that the next technician will read. It also gives you the four readings that tell you which stage failed when one comes back bad.

4 sections  ·  10 topics  ·  11 activities

By the end of Lesson 529.4 you will be able to
  • Identify the Power over Ethernet (PoE) class and power budget for the device.
  • Patch the device through the installed link.
  • Verify PoE delivery at the switch and the device.
  • Confirm link speed and duplex.
  • Verify the device reaches the network.
  • Record the connection on the port map.

Two numbers decide whether a powered device will run on a given port. They are usually different, and treating them as one is how a switch runs out of power with half its ports empty.

A switch supplying Power over Ethernet does not energize a port and hope. It tests the port, finds out what is on the far end, asks how much power that device needs, and sets that much aside from a shared pool before it supplies anything. The device then draws what it draws.

So there are two figures on one link, and they belong to different parties.

  • What the device draws. The figure on its specification sheet.
  • What the port sets aside. Normally a number the switch picks from a short list, based on the class the device reports, and less where the device negotiates for less. This is what comes out of the pool.

The reservation is normally the larger, and it is the one that governs how many devices a switch can carry.

A closing line reads: the 9.0 W the device draws never appears in this line; the reservation does.

The classes, and what each one costs the pool

The class is a bracket rather than a measurement, so a device drawing 7.1 W and a device drawing 12.9 W both land in class 3 and both cost the pool the same 15.4 W. Read a stated draw against the class 1 to 4 brackets below and you have the class the device should declare. Class 0 is not a bracket you read a draw into — it is what the port assumes when the device declares nothing.

Power classes, read from both ends of the link.
ClassWhat the device may drawWhat the port sets asideDevices that usually land here
0Any draw from 0.44 to 12.95 W15.4 WThe default, where a device presents no classification signature
10.44 to 3.84 W4.0 WLow-draw sensors and card readers
2above 3.84 to 6.49 W7.0 WDesk phones, fixed indoor cameras
3above 6.49 to 12.95 W15.4 WIndoor access points, feature phones
4above 12.95 to 25.5 W30.0 WOutdoor cameras with heaters, higher-draw access points

Classes 0 through 3 are defined by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) in 802.3af. Class 4 arrives with 802.3at. An 802.3af port cannot run a class 4 device however much the pool has left: it supplies up to its own ceiling, which is less than the device needs. The device may fail to start, may start and restart, or may run with some functions off.

The class is reported, not measured

The device states its class during classification. That is usually consistent with what it draws, but a manufacturer may report a higher class to leave headroom for a later firmware release or an attached accessory.

So the class off a specification sheet is a planning figure; the class the switch reports is the device in front of you. Where they disagree, believe the switch.

Some switches trim the reservation afterward

On some equipment the initial reservation is the class figure. The two ends then exchange a more precise requirement, and the switch reduces what it holds. On other equipment the class figure stands for as long as the device is connected.

Size a job on the class figure either way: it is the worst case.

529.4.2 Reading power from a specification sheet

A specification sheet lists what a device needs. Three of its lines cover power, usually in a block headed Hardware or Electrical.

The power lines from one indoor access point’s specification sheet.
LineValueWhat it tells you
Power methodPoEThe device takes its power from the cabling. If this line reads direct current only, or lists a power adapter, then a powered port will not run it and the device needs an outlet
Supported voltage range44 to 57 V direct current (DC)The band the device expects at its input. A port supplies 44 to 57 V, and a compliant device runs down to about 37 V, because the cable loses some of the voltage on the way. A band well away from what a standards-compliant port supplies points to a proprietary product
Maximum power consumption9 WThe figure to compare against what the port can deliver to a device, and the figure whose bracket gives you the class. 9 W sits above 6.49 W and below 12.95 W, so it falls in the class 3 bracket

Those three lines give you what the device draws and the class bracket that draw falls in. What the port sets aside follows from the class.

Four things a specification sheet does not always make easy

  • The class is often not printed. Many specification sheets give a wattage and no class. Read the wattage against the class brackets and take the class from that.
  • “PoE” on its own is not a specification. A sheet that says PoE and gives no wattage and no class has not answered the question. Ask the manufacturer for the figure rather than assume the lowest class.
  • “PoE+” means class 4 may be needed. Treat it as a warning that an 802.3af port may not run the device, and confirm the wattage before the device is ordered.
  • Maximum is not typical. The stated maximum is what the device may pull with everything on it working hard. Plan on the maximum; a device that only occasionally reaches it will still bring the switch down when several devices reach it together.
Two ceilings, and the device has to clear both

The port ceiling is the most one port will supply: 15.4 W on an 802.3af port, 30.0 W on an 802.3at port. No setting raises it. Not all of that reaches the device — up to 100 meters of cable loses some of it on the way — so the standard allows a device to consume at most 12.95 W on an 802.3af port and 25.5 W on an 802.3at port. That is the figure to compare a device’s stated draw against. A device whose maximum draw sits above it may not start, may start and restart, or may run with some functions off — it varies by model.

The pool is shared across all ports and runs out independently of the port ceiling, so a device can clear the ceiling and still stay dark.

Where the ceiling and the pool are written down

The switch states its own per-port ceiling in the Max column of show power inline, and its pool on the header line of the same output. Read both from the switch in front of you — a rack holds switches of different ages that supply different amounts.

529.4.3 Sizing the switch against a work order

A work order names devices. The switch has a pool. Getting from one to the other is addition, and the common mistake is adding the wrong column.

A work order lists twelve powered devices for one telecommunications room. You can settle whether they fit before anybody climbs a ladder, and settling it late is expensive: the last devices installed are the ones that stay dark.

Start by asking the switch what it has left.

show power inline — the header line
Switch# show power inline

Available:370.0(w)  Used:19.4(w)  Remaining:350.6(w)

Available is the whole pool, Used is what is already held for ports with powered devices on them, and Remaining is what is left to allocate. For a new work order, read Remaining rather than Available — the ports already in service are not going to give their allocation back. Run the command without naming a port and it prints this header line first, then one output row for each port.

Then read the specification sheets and add the reservations.

Twelve devices from one work order, sized against 350.6 W remaining.
DeviceQtyStated maximum drawClass the draw falls inSet aside, eachSet aside, total
Indoor access point69.0 W315.4 W92.4 W
Desk phone45.2 W27.0 W28.0 W
Outdoor camera221.5 W430.0 W60.0 W
Total held from the pool12———180.4 W

180.4 W against 350.6 W remaining leaves 170.2 W, so the work order fits and there is room for the next one. The switch also needs 802.3at for the two class 4 cameras, and twelve free ports.

Adding the draws gives you a different answer, and it is the wrong one

The same twelve devices draw 54.0 W, 20.8 W and 43.0 W, for 117.8 W. Adding those gives 232.8 W left rather than 170.2 W — an overstatement of 62.6 W, which is enough reservation for four more class 3 access points.

The switch holds the reservation, not the draw. Add the column the switch is working from.

Leave the pool some room

A switch at its limit does not fail politely. Ports lose power in an order nobody chose, and a device that has run for months can go dark when an unrelated one is added at the other end of the rack.

Sizing to the last watt also leaves nothing for a change of use. Leave a quarter of the pool unallocated, unless the site has its own design standard.

Match each stated maximum draw to the class whose bracket it falls in.

529.4.4 Patching the device through the link

Two patch cords complete the path. They are the two elements a person handles, and the only two that can change after the run is qualified — tested and recorded as meeting the standard it was installed to.

The horizontal cable, the outlet and the panel termination are fixed work. The two patch cords are different: anybody can move them, and they are short enough to be handled casually. Give a cord the same care as a termination.

Before you handle a cord

Three facts, all off the work order and the port map, before you touch anything.

  • Which panel position the drop terminates at. Fixed by the installation, and printed on the panel.
  • Which switch port the drop is to be patched to. This is the port that has been configured: the Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) assignment and the power setting were made on one specific port. The port beside it carries only defaults.
  • Which outlet the device sits at. Fixed by the installation, and printed on the faceplate.

Patching one port off is a common mistake here, and a quiet one. A default port still supplies power, so the device comes up and looks installed while sitting in the wrong VLAN. To the user that looks like a network problem, not a patching error.

The cord itself

Check four things on the cord first.

What to check on a patch cord before it goes in.
CheckWhat good looks likeWhat it costs to get wrong
CategoryThe cord matches the run’s category or beats it. A link is only as good as its weakest elementA lower-category cord holds the whole link down to its own limit, and the run gets blamed
ConditionLatch intact, boot intact, jacket unbroken, no kinks and no sharp bendsA broken latch works today and works loose later — the fault that comes and goes

The console port is not a network port

The console socket is the same RJ45 shape as every network port, and the printed word above it is the only difference. A cord in it carries serial data to the switch’s command line — that is what the socket is for — but no Ethernet traffic and no power, so your configured port reports nothing connected.

Read the label above the socket before the cord goes in.

Working in a live rack

Every cord already in the panel is carrying somebody’s service. Move one to make room and that service stops, without warning and without anybody connecting it to what you were doing.

To free a position, find out what is on it and get the change agreed. Put a cord you remove back in the same position; one port along is a fault somebody else finds days later.

529.4.5 Power, read at both ends

The switch says what it is supplying. The device shows what it is receiving. Taken together they separate a supply problem from a device problem, which neither does alone.

The switch end gives you numbers. The device end gives you behavior.

At the switch

Ask about the one port you patched, not all of them.

show power inline, for one port
Switch# show power inline gigabitethernet 1/0/12

Interface Admin  Oper       Power   Device              Class Max
                            (Watts)
--------- ------ ---------- ------- ------------------- ----- ----
Gi1/0/12  auto   on         15.4    Ieee PD             3     30.0

Name the port in the form the switch uses, which may be fastethernet rather than gigabitethernet. The output row then reads: Admin auto — power is permitted. Oper on — power is flowing. 15.4 W — the power the switch has set aside for this device. Not a measurement of what it is pulling. Ieee PD — a standards-compliant powered device, not something unrecognized. Class 3 — what the device asked for, which is where the 15.4 W comes from. Max 30.0 — the ceiling of the port, not of the device. It reads the same whatever is plugged in.

That one output row answers the power half of the job on its own, and it is the switch reporting on its own port.

The committed figure and the drawn figure answer different questions

The Power column is what the switch has set aside, not what it is measuring. Where the device says nothing about its needs, the switch sets aside the class figure — 15.4 W for class 3. Where the device negotiates over Cisco Discovery Protocol (CDP) or Link Layer Discovery Protocol (LLDP) — two ways a device and a switch tell each other what they are — the switch sets aside the smaller figure the device asked for.

Either way the reading confirms the switch granted power and the device accepted it. To see what the device is actually pulling, show power inline police reports the measured figure for the port alongside the committed one.

At the device

What the device tells you depends on the device. Many have a web page or a management system of their own, though reaching one needs its Internet Protocol (IP) address, credentials and permission you may not have. What you can always read is its behavior.

Five combinations of switch reading and device behavior cover almost everything you will meet.

529.4.6 Confirming speed and duplex

A link that is up is not a link that is right. Speed and duplex are where a run that passed its wire map admits that it did not pass everything.

In autonegotiation, two ports tell each other which speeds they support and whether they can send and receive at once, then settle on the best pair both support. It runs on its own unless somebody sets a fixed value instead.

Two words describe the result.

  • Speed is how fast the link runs: 10, 100 or 1000 megabits per second (Mbps) on the copper ports you will meet.
  • Duplex is whether the two ends can transmit at the same time. Full duplex means they can. Half duplex means they take turns, so the two directions share the capacity instead of each having it. Collisions under load are normal on a half-duplex link, not a fault. What points at a mismatch is late collisions, counted on the port.
show interfaces status
Switch# show interfaces status

Port      Name          Status       Vlan    Duplex  Speed Type
Gi1/0/12                connected    20        a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/14                connected    20        a-full  a-100 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/16                connected    20        a-half  a-100 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/18                connected    20          full   1000 10/100/1000BaseTX

The a- in front of a value means this port is set to negotiate rather than to a fixed setting. Gi1/0/12 negotiated full duplex at 1000 Mbps, which is what a gigabit-capable device on a sound gigabit run should reach. Gi1/0/14 negotiated full duplex at 100 Mbps. Gi1/0/16 is set to negotiate and has ended at half duplex. Gi1/0/18 shows full and 1000 with no a- at all, so this port was set to fixed values and is not negotiating. Name is a label somebody can write on a port and is empty on all four here. Type is what the port itself is built for — 10/100/1000BaseTX means it can run at any of the three speeds over copper, which is why the Speed column is worth reading rather than assumed.

What reports a duplex mismatch, and what does not

A mismatched link comes up, passes traffic, and lights both indicators. It runs slowly and loses frames under load, which the people using it report as a slow network rather than a fault.

The switch says so when it can. Where CDP or LLDP runs on both ends, the switch logs the port and the mismatch. The message comes from CDP or LLDP, not from the negotiation, and neither protocol fixes what it reports.

Where neither is running, nothing is logged. The two ends have no way to compare settings, so nothing announces it. What is left is the Duplex column, and the error counters climbing behind it: late collisions and frame-check errors on the half-duplex end, undersized frames on the full-duplex end. A late collision is one detected after the frame should already have been clear of the wire, and it is the counter that means a mismatch rather than ordinary traffic. show interfaces <port> prints the counters for one port. Reading the Duplex column when you install the device catches it either way, and it takes one command.

Why a fault can hide until the speed goes up

All four pairs are terminated at both ends. How many the link uses depends on the speed.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram is headed Which pairs carry the data, and compares two speeds side by side, separated by a vertical dashed line.

On the left, headed 100 Mbps, two pairs carry data. The four pairs are listed by the pins they land on: pins 1 and 2, pins 3 and 6, pins 4 and 5, and pins 7 and 8. Each is drawn as a horizontal bar. The bars for pins 1 and 2 and for pins 3 and 6 are solid blue and labeled carries data. The bars for pins 4 and 5 and for pins 7 and 8 are pale gray and labeled not used for data.

On the right, headed 1000 Mbps, all four pairs carry data. The same four pairs are listed by the same pins, and all four bars are solid blue and labeled carries data.

A panel beneath is headed A fault on the 4/5 or the 7/8 pair, and reads: no data crosses those two pairs at 100 Mbps, so the link comes up and works; at 1000 Mbps the same fault stops the link reaching that speed.

A second panel is headed What this means for a reading, and states that a gigabit-capable device negotiating 100 Mbps is reporting a path problem on a run that passes a wire map.

All four pairs have to be correct to qualify, so a wire map taken at hand-over would have found a fault on 4/5 or 7/8. Two things get past it. The fault arrived after the test — a re-termination, a crushed cord, somebody working in the ceiling. Or the pair is wired correctly and performs badly: crosstalk, return loss and delay skew pass a wire map and fail a certification. A verifier reports how the cabling is built; only a certifier measures how it performs. Either way the run carries 100 Mbps, fails at 1000, and nobody finds out until a gigabit device is installed.

Those pairs are not idle on a powered link

A port puts its current either on the 1/2 and 3/6 pairs or on the 4/5 and 7/8 pairs. The switch chooses and the device accepts either. At 10 and 100 Mbps only 1/2 and 3/6 carry data, so the other two are free; at 1000 Mbps all four carry data and the power rides along with it.

So a fault on 4/5 or 7/8 can be invisible at 100 Mbps and still stop power. Take both readings.

529.4.7 Reaching the network, and where your part ends

Powered and linked is not the same as reachable. The last check is whether something else on the same network can get a reply out of the device — and that reply also settles whose problem it is.

Reachable is a separate fact and it needs a separate check. At this level, two facts settle it.

  1. The switch has heard from the device. A Media Access Control (MAC) address learned on your port is proof that the device sent a frame and that the frame reached the switch across the cabling. It does not prove the cabling is sound at every speed — a fault on one pair still carries frames at 100 Mbps, which is why you read the Speed column beside it. show mac address-table interface for that port is what returns it.
  2. A laptop in the same VLAN gets a reply from the device. This is the check you run, and a reply also tells you the device holds a working IP address in that range — you do not check that separately.
show mac address-table interface
Switch# show mac address-table interface gigabitethernet 1/0/12
          Mac Address Table
-------------------------------------------

Vlan    Mac Address       Type        Ports
----    -----------       --------    -----
  20    7483.c21a.9f04    DYNAMIC     Gi1/0/12

One line, four columns. The device’s MAC address is written by the manufacturer and printed here in the three-block form the switch uses. Vlan is the group the switch put the port in — read it against the work order. Type reads DYNAMIC because the switch learned it from a frame rather than being told it. Ports is the port the frame arrived on. An empty result means the switch has heard nothing on that port recently — entries are dropped after a few minutes of silence, so re-read it rather than treating one empty result as proof.

The check

A ping checks whether a machine at a given IP address is reachable from where you run it. Where you run it from is a choice, and on a drop you have just brought up the choice is a laptop in the same VLAN as the device.

That way the ping never leaves the switch, and what it tests is the cabling, the port and the device, and nothing else. A ping to a device in another VLAN passes through a router on the way, which adds a cause you do not own. Across a router a ping is strong evidence when it succeeds and weak evidence when it fails.

Two things have to be in place before you can run it. The device’s IP address comes from the work order; where the work order does not carry it, ask for it rather than guess. A laptop in the device’s VLAN is arranged with the network team before the visit, because you cannot put yourself in a VLAN from the desk.

Where you have neither, say so and stop there. The MAC address learned on the port is the last reading you own, and it is enough to hand over on: it shows the device is connected, powered and heard by the switch, and it leaves the addressing to whoever owns it.

What a timeout does not prove

Some devices never answer a ping, and a timeout from one of those is correct behavior. Cameras and building controllers often ship that way.

That is why you take the MAC address table reading as well. A MAC address with no ping reply means the device is on the network and declining to answer, not that it is dead.

Where your part of the path ends

Where a device is powered, linked and heard from and still cannot reach what it needs, the fault is past the part of the path you own.

529.4.8 Recording the connection

The device will outlast the ticket, the ticket will outlast your memory of it, and the record is what remains. A record made of readings can be checked; a record made of opinions cannot.

What a record needs besides the readings

A date and a name. A reading without a date cannot be compared with a later one, and a record nobody signed cannot be asked about.

Anything you left different from what the work order said. A drop patched to a different port from the one specified, a cord replaced, a position found already occupied — all of that goes in the record. A substitution that is written down is a decision; the same substitution unrecorded is a fault waiting to be found.

Where you may write, and where you may not

On many sites a technician can read the port map but changes go through whoever owns it. Settle that before you start: where the record is, whether you may write to it, and who updates it if you may not.

Where you may not write to it, hand the readings over anyway. The port, the VLAN, the four readings, the date and your name is what the record owner needs, and producing it is part of the job.

529.4.9 Reading the four checks together

Each check answers one question. Read together, they eliminate causes until one is left.

Every good reading moves the search further from the cabling and closer to the device or the network.

The four checks, and what each one rules out when it comes back good.

These four describe the connection as it stands. Whether the port carries the settings the work order specified is a separate question, answered by reading the configuration back.

Take all four before you change anything

The readings describe the state you found. Swap a cord or replace a device and that state is gone, along with the evidence for whatever you conclude afterward.

A good reading is not always good news

A fault on a pair that 100 Mbps does not use still lets the link come up, the MAC address appear, and the ping answer. Three checks pass and the link is still not what the work order asked for.

The Speed column is what catches it: the link settled at 100 Mbps because the pair the higher speed needs is faulty. That is why speed and duplex are read rather than assumed from the link being up.

Lesson 529.4 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

529.4.10 What you covered

Size the device to the port, patch it, take four readings, and write down what they said.

Two figures describe power on one link, and the reservation is normally the larger. What the device draws is on its specification sheet. What the port sets aside is fixed by the class the device reports, and that is what comes out of the shared pool. A device drawing 9.0 W and reporting class 3 costs the pool 15.4 W. Size a job by adding the reservations; adding the draws gives a larger remainder than the switch has.

A device has to clear two ceilings. The port ceiling is 15.4 W on an 802.3af port and 30.0 W on an 802.3at port, and no setting raises it. The pool is shared across all ports and runs out on its own. Read both from the switch in front of you: the pool on the header line of show power inline, the ceiling in the Max column. Size against Remaining, not Available, and leave a quarter unallocated unless the site standard says otherwise.

Three lines on a specification sheet cover power. Power method says whether the device takes power from the cabling. Supported voltage range gives the band it expects. Maximum power consumption is what you compare against what the port can deliver to a device, and its bracket gives the class. A sheet that says only PoE and no wattage has not told you enough.

Two patch cords complete the path, and they are the only two elements a person handles. Settle the panel position, the switch port and the outlet from the record before touching a cord. The port beside the configured one carries only defaults, and a default port still supplies power — so the device may come up in the wrong VLAN rather than not at all. Check category, condition, length and seating, and read the label above the socket — the console port is the same shape and a cord in it does nothing.

Verify — four readings, taken before anything is changed
show power inline <port>                 ! permitted, flowing, how much, what class, port ceiling
show interfaces status                   ! connected, in the VLAN, at what speed and duplex
show mac address-table interface <port>  ! has a device been heard on this port
ping <device IP address>                 ! from a laptop in the device’s VLAN

Take all four before changing anything. A cord swapped first removes the evidence for whatever you conclude afterward. You type only the part in bold: the text after each ! is a note for you, and a word in angle brackets is where your own value goes.

On the power reading, Admin is what the port may do and Oper is what it is doing. Admin never with Oper off is a setting. Admin auto with Oper off means one of two things: nothing was detected on the far end, or the pool is spent. The header line tells you which, by whether any power remains. Oper on with a dark device puts the fault in the device. The Power column is what the switch has set aside, not what it is measuring.

On the link reading, an a- prefix means the port negotiates; no prefix means it is fixed. a-full a-1000 is what a gigabit device on a sound run gives. a-half where full was expected means either a genuinely half-duplex far end or one forced to a setting and not negotiating. Record and report whichever you find rather than undoing somebody’s setting. Where CDP or LLDP runs on both ends the switch logs the mismatch; where neither runs, the Duplex column is what you read instead. A gigabit device at a-100 points to the path: 100 Mbps uses only the 1/2 and 3/6 pairs, so a fault on 4/5 or 7/8 hides until the speed goes up — and the power may be riding on those same pairs.

Two facts settle reachability: the switch has learned the device’s MAC address on your port, and a laptop in the same VLAN gets a reply from the device. That reply also shows the device holds a working IP address in that range. A timeout is a reading, not a diagnosis — some devices are configured never to answer a ping, which is why you read the MAC address table beside it.

Where this stops

One VLAN carries one IP address range. Same VLAN and same range, they talk. A different VLAN is reached through a router, and routing and addressing sit with whoever configures the router.

A device that is powered, negotiated, in the right VLAN and heard from by the switch has cleared everything the cabling and the port can be asked for. The rest is theirs, and your four readings are what they need.

The port map outlasts the visit. Three identifiers describe the drop — outlet, panel position, switch port — and the switch port is the one that changes. The four readings, a date and a name make that row something the next technician can compare against what the port reports on the day. “Working” is an opinion. “Class 3 with 15.4 W committed, connected at full duplex and 1000 Mbps, 4 of 4 replies” is a state.
