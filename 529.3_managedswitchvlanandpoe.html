529.3.1 One switch, more than one network

Lesson 529.3

Managed Switch VLAN and PoE Configuration

A managed switch is configured by typing commands into it, one line at a time. Bringing up a single port is a short, fixed sequence: create the group the device belongs to, put the port into that group, allow it to supply power over the cable, and switch it on. This lesson gives you that sequence and what each line does, along with the commands that prove the port is working rather than assume it. It also gives you the one command that keeps the work through the next power cut, and a written boundary marking where switch configuration stops being your job.

4 sections  ·  10 topics  ·  11 activities

By the end of Lesson 529.3 you will be able to
  • Explain what a Virtual Local Area Network (VLAN) does to a switch’s broadcast domains.
  • Connect to the switch console to reach the command line.
  • Create a VLAN for data or device separation.
  • Assign access ports to the VLAN.
  • Enable Power over Ethernet (PoE) on a port.
  • Verify link status and PoE delivery.
  • Save the running configuration.
  • Identify the switch configuration tasks that belong to the network team.

Out of the box, every port on a switch is in the same group. Most buildings need more groups than that, and buying more switches is not how you get them.

Until its ports are assigned to VLANs, a switch keeps all of them in one group. Plug in eight devices and all eight can reach each other. Two terms describe what that traffic is. A single unit of data on the local network is a frame. A frame addressed to everyone at once is a broadcast, and a broadcast arriving on any port goes out of every other port on the switch. The set of ports that receive a broadcast is a broadcast domain, and by default the switch is one broadcast domain.

That is fine for eight devices. It stops being fine at a scale you meet in almost every commercial building, and for three separate reasons.

  • Volume. Devices announce themselves constantly. A few dozen devices produce background chatter nobody notices. A few hundred in one broadcast domain produce enough that every device on it spends real effort receiving and discarding traffic meant for somebody else.
  • Reach. Anything in one broadcast domain can attempt to talk to anything else in it. A door controller, a camera, a payment terminal, and a guest laptop sharing one domain is a design nobody would choose on purpose.
  • Blast radius. A misbehaving device floods the domain it is in. If that domain is the whole building, the whole building slows down.

All three problems have the same fix: stop putting every device in one broadcast domain. The obvious way to do that is physical. Give each group of devices its own switch, and the groups are separated because there is no wire between them. It works, and it is how small separated networks are still built. It is also expensive, it wastes ports, and it means a change of use at one desk becomes a cabling job.

A VLAN produces the same separation as a setting rather than as a purchase. You tell the switch which group each port belongs to. Ports in one group exchange frames with each other and receive each other’s broadcasts. Ports in a different group do the same among themselves, and nothing passes between the two groups. One physical switch behaves as though it were several separate switches, and the boundary between them is a line in the configuration rather than a gap in the wiring.

Open each card below. The separation a VLAN produces is narrower than it first appears, and knowing what it leaves untouched is what keeps you from reaching for it as an explanation when something else is wrong.

The distinction that decides who owns the problem

In a correctly built network, one VLAN carries one Internet Protocol (IP) address range. Two devices in the same VLAN, with addresses in that range, can talk to each other. Two devices in different VLANs cannot, and connecting them is a router’s job rather than a switch’s. That single distinction decides, more often than any other, whether a connectivity problem in front of you is yours to fix or somebody else’s to answer for.

529.3.2 Getting to the command line

A managed switch has no screen and no keyboard of its own. Your laptop supplies both, joined to the switch by a cable that carries your keystrokes over and its replies back.

A switch out of its box has no IP address, no password, and no way to be reached over the network, because the network is the thing you have not configured yet. What it does have is a console port: a dedicated socket whose only purpose is to accept a terminal.

The console connection is not a network connection. It carries no network traffic and needs no IP addressing, so it keeps working on a switch whose network settings are wrong or whose ports are all shut down. It is also the only way to watch the switch start up. That is what it is for.

What the connection needs

Three things: a physical path from your laptop to the console port, a terminal program on the laptop, and the right serial settings.

The serial settings

The terminal and the switch have to agree on how fast to talk and how to frame each character. One combination is near-universal on network equipment, and it is worth memorizing because there is no way to discover it from the far end.

A blank window is not a failed connection

Once the terminal opens, the window is often empty. The switch has nothing to say until you say something. Press Enter and a prompt appears.

If pressing Enter produces nothing, work through the list in order: the cable seated at both ends, the correct serial port chosen, the speed set to 9600, and flow control off. If characters appear but arrive as unreadable symbols, the two ends disagree on how to frame them — the speed is wrong, or less often the data bits or parity. Start with the speed.

The first prompt

A switch that has just been powered on prints its startup messages to the console as it goes: the model it is, the software it is running, and the state of its interfaces. The earliest of those messages appear on the console as they happen and are gone once they have scrolled past — the switch only starts keeping a copy of its messages later in the startup. That is the reason for connecting before you power a switch on rather than after.

Decline the setup dialog

The initial configuration dialog asks a series of questions and writes a configuration from the answers. It is not how the work in this lesson is done, and it does not ask about VLANs or Power over Ethernet at all.

Answer n and you are left at the command line, where every setting is made deliberately and one line at a time. The dialog is offered only on a switch with nothing saved, so on a switch already in service you will not see it.

What you are left with is short, and it tells you three things.

Console session, first contact
Switch>

Switch is the device name, unchanged from the factory. The > says you are in the lowest privilege level. The cursor after it says the switch is listening.

That is a working command line. Everything else in this lesson happens from here.

If the switch is reached over the network instead

A switch that has already been given a management IP address and had network access enabled can be reached with the same terminal program over the network, rather than through the console port. You select a network connection instead of a serial one, enter the switch’s IP address, and sign in.

What is identical. Everything after the prompt appears. The modes, the commands, the verification, and the save are exactly the same, and nothing in the rest of this lesson changes.

What is not. A network connection depends on the switch already being configured correctly — it has an IP address, its management path is up, and your laptop can reach it. Break any of those from the command line and the session you are typing in disappears with them. The console connection has no such dependency, which is why it stays the method of last resort and the method used on a switch that has never been configured.

529.3.3 Where you are, and what you can type there

The command line accepts different commands depending on where in it you are standing. The prompt tells you where that is, and reading it is the skill.

A switch command line is not one flat list of commands. It is a set of modes, each holding the commands that make sense in it. A command the switch does not recognize in the mode you are in is rejected — not because the command is wrong, but because it does not belong where you are.

This is the single most common source of confusion for somebody new to a switch. The prompt always states the mode. It is not decoration.

Text description of this diagram

The diagram is a ladder of five command line modes, drawn top to bottom. Every command is shown in a small box in monospace type; a key at the foot of the diagram states that boxed monospace text is a command you type. Commands on the left of the ladder point downward and enter a mode. Commands on the right point upward and leave one.

  1. User EXEC, described as look but change nothing, displays the prompt Switch>.
  2. Typing enable moves down to Privileged EXEC, described as read everything, save, restart, displaying the prompt Switch#. Typing disable moves back up.
  3. Typing configure terminal moves down to Global configuration, described as change settings for the whole switch, displaying the prompt Switch(config)#. Typing end returns upward.
  4. From global configuration, two branches lead downward. Typing vlan followed by a number enters VLAN configuration, settings for one VLAN, with the prompt (config-vlan)#. Typing interface followed by a port name enters Interface configuration, settings for one port, with the prompt (config-if)#.

Reading the five prompts

Each prompt has two parts: the device name, and a symbol or bracketed word saying what you may do.

The five modes used in this lesson, how you enter each one, and what it is for.
PromptModeCommand that enters itWhat you can do there
Switch>User EXECNone — a console session opens hereA small set of look-only commands. Where a session starts. You cannot change or even fully read the configuration from here
Switch#Privileged EXECenableEvery show command, saving the configuration, and restarting the switch. This is where you verify work and where you save it
Switch(config)#Global configurationconfigure terminalSettings that apply to the switch as a whole, including creating a VLAN and choosing which port to work on next
Switch(config-vlan)#VLAN configurationvlan <number>Settings for the one VLAN you named on the way in, such as its name. Everything typed here applies to that VLAN and nothing else

Read the bracket before you type

Switch(config)# and Switch(config-if)# differ by three characters and by everything that matters. The first applies to the whole switch. The second applies to one port. Watch for a command that is valid in both, such as shutdown: it is accepted wherever you type it, and applied to whatever that mode governs — the whole switch, or the one port. Read the prompt before you press Enter.

The habit that prevents this costs nothing: look at the prompt, then type. Every time.

Three things the command line does to help

  • Abbreviation. A command can be shortened as long as what you type is unambiguous. conf t is accepted for configure terminal, and sh ru for show running-config. Read abbreviations comfortably, because other people use them; type commands in full while you are learning, because a full command is one you can also read back.
  • The question mark. Typing ? lists what is valid at that point. On its own it lists every command available in the current mode. After a partial command it lists the ways that command can be completed. It is the fastest way to answer “what am I allowed to type here” without guessing.
  • The Tab key. Completes a command you have started typing enough of. Useful, and a quiet check on your spelling: nothing completes if the word is wrong.
Two messages, two different problems

% Invalid input detected at '^' marker means the switch did not recognize what you typed at the point the caret marks. The usual causes are a typing error, or a command that does not exist in the mode you are in.

% Incomplete command means the command was recognized but is missing something — a number, a name, an interface. Press the up arrow to bring the line back, add a space and a question mark, and the switch lists what is missing.

Neither message changes anything on the switch. A rejected command is a command that did not run.

Match each prompt to the mode it announces.

529.3.4 Naming the port you are about to configure

Before you can configure a port you have to name it the way the switch names it — and the switch in front of you may not use the same form as the one beside it.

A work order names a port by where it is: the twelfth port on the switch in the second-floor room. The switch names it differently, and the two have to be reconciled before anything can be typed. Get the name wrong and one of two things happens. Either the switch rejects it, which is harmless, or it accepts it and you have configured a port belonging to somebody else, which is not.

Text description of this diagram

The interface name GigabitEthernet1/0/12 is drawn along the top as four boxed segments, each with a numbered marker beneath it. The two forward slashes sit between the last three segments, and nothing separates the port type from the first number. A note states that the name is one unbroken string, split only to show its parts, and that you do not type spaces. The same four numbers key the list below, so each part can be matched to its description without relying on color.

  1. GigabitEthernet is the port type — the speed family of this port.
  2. 1 is the stack member: which switch, when several are joined and managed as one. A switch on its own is number 1.
  3. 0 is the module: which card in the switch. Fixed-port switches use 0.
  4. 12 is the port number: the socket itself, numbered as printed on the front of the switch.

A strip at the foot gives the short forms: GigabitEthernet1/0/12 is written Gi1/0/12, and FastEthernet1/0/12 is written Fa1/0/12.

Two port types, and why it matters which one you are looking at

Switch ports come in two roles. The access ports are the numbered sockets across the front, and a work area drop is patched to one of them. The uplink ports are the few at the end that carry the switch’s traffic onward to the rest of the network. A desk drop lands on an access port, and an access port is the only kind you configure.

The first part of the interface name is the port type, and it states the speed family the port belongs to.

The two port types you will meet on switches of this kind.
Written in fullShortSpeedNotes
FastEthernetFa10 or 100 MbpsFound on the access ports of older switches, including models that supply Power over Ethernet
GigabitEthernetGi10, 100 or 1000 MbpsFound on the access ports of newer switches, and on the uplink ports of almost all of them
Do not assume the type — confirm it

Two switches sitting in the same rack can name their ports differently. A switch whose access ports are FastEthernet will reject interface gigabitethernet 1/0/12, because it has no such port. The reverse is equally true.

It is also common for one switch to use both: FastEthernet access ports across the front, and GigabitEthernet uplink ports at the end. The uplink is not where a desk gets patched, so the type you need is whatever the access ports use.

Confirm the type on the switch you are actually at, every time, before you type an interface command. It takes one command and it removes the whole problem.

Confirming it from the switch

One command lists every port with its name, its state, and the VLAN it is in. The Port column on the left is the switch telling you what it calls its own ports, which is the only opinion that counts.

Privileged EXEC — list the ports and their names
Switch# show interfaces status

Port      Name          Status       Vlan    Duplex  Speed Type
Fa1/0/10                notconnect   1         auto   auto 10/100BaseTX
Fa1/0/11                notconnect   1         auto   auto 10/100BaseTX
Fa1/0/12                notconnect   1         auto   auto 10/100BaseTX
Fa1/0/13                notconnect   1         auto   auto 10/100BaseTX
Gi1/0/1                 notconnect   1         auto   auto unknown

The access ports on this switch are Fa, and the Type column confirms why: 10/100BaseTX, meaning the port runs at 10 or 100 Mbps over copper. Gi1/0/1 at the bottom is an uplink, and its Type reads unknown because nothing is plugged into it yet — that socket takes a module, and the switch cannot say what kind until one is fitted. Name is a label somebody can write on a port and is empty on all five. Duplex and Speed read auto because no link has formed for them to settle. On this switch, an interface command has to say fastethernet.

Two other commands answer the same question from a different angle, and each also tells you what you are standing in front of.

  • show version reports the model and the software running on it, along with a count of how many ports of each type the switch has.
  • show inventory reports the model number and serial number of the switch and of anything plugged into it.
Reading a model number

Switch model numbers state the port configuration if you know where to look. In a model number such as WS-C3750-48PS-S, the number is the access port count and the letter straight after it describes those ports: P for Power over Ethernet, T for plain copper with no power. An S in that same group indicates Small Form-factor Pluggable (SFP) uplink sockets — slots that take a plug-in fiber or copper module — and a G in the family name indicates Gigabit access ports.

So 48PS is forty-eight ports with power, and 24TS is twenty-four without. You can read this off a label, and it is not a substitute for show interfaces status — read the label to know what to expect, and read the switch to know what is true.

529.3.5 Creating the VLAN and assigning the port

Two jobs, done in one visit to the command line: bring the VLAN into existence, then put a port into it.

Creating a VLAN and putting a port into it are two separate jobs, and each is done in a different mode. A VLAN belongs to the switch, so it is created in global configuration. A port assignment belongs to one port, so it is made in that port’s own configuration mode. Assign a port to a VLAN that has not been created and the switch creates it for you, unnamed and undescribed. That works, and it leaves the next technician a bare number with nothing attached to it. Create the VLAN first, and name it.

Part one — create the VLAN and name it

Creating VLAN 20
Switch> enable
Switch# configure terminal
Switch(config)# vlan 20
Switch(config-vlan)# name DATA-20
Switch(config-vlan)# exit
Switch(config)#

Watch the prompt change at each step. It moved from > to #, into (config), into (config-vlan), and back out to where the port work begins.

Naming is not decoration

A VLAN with no name still works. It also arrives at the next technician as a bare number with no indication of what it is for. Nobody will then move a port into or out of it without asking somebody first, and the person who could answer has often moved on.

Names are short, upper case by convention, and describe the group rather than the room: DATA-20, VOICE-25, CAMERA-30. Whatever scheme the site already uses beats whatever scheme you would have chosen.

Part two — put the port in it

Now name the port, in the form the switch uses, and give it three settings.

Assigning a port to VLAN 20
Switch(config)# interface gigabitethernet 1/0/12   ! or fastethernet, per the switch
Switch(config-if)# switchport mode access
Switch(config-if)# switchport access vlan 20
Switch(config-if)# no shutdown
Switch(config-if)# end
Switch#

end rather than exit at the close. exit would step back to (config)#; end leaves configuration altogether and returns you to Switch#, which is where verification happens.

What each command in part two does.
CommandWhat it does

Why no shutdown is in the sequence even when the port is already up

On most switches an access port is enabled by default, so on a port nobody has touched, this command changes nothing.

It is in the sequence because you are rarely the first person at the port. Disabling a port is a normal thing to do when a desk is vacated, and it leaves no trace at the desk end: no light, no link, and a user reporting a broken cable. Typing no shutdown as part of the standard sequence removes that possibility for the cost of one command.

On this equipment, prefixing a command with no undoes it. shutdown disables the port and no shutdown re-enables it. The same pattern applies throughout: no switchport access vlan 20 would return the port to the default VLAN.

The port takes effect the moment you press Enter

There is no apply step and no confirmation. A configuration line runs when you press Enter, and if a device is connected to that port, the change reaches it immediately.

This is why the port number is checked against the work order before the interface line is typed rather than after. Configuring the wrong port disconnects whoever is on it, and they will find out before you do.

One more command belongs inside the port’s own configuration mode, alongside the three settings already there. It is the subject of the next topic.

529.3.6 Allowing the port to supply power

A port on a PoE-capable switch is already allowed to supply power; that is how it leaves the factory. One command states that permission, changes it, or withdraws it.

On a Power over Ethernet link the switch port supplies the power and the connected device consumes it. The supplying end is the PSE, power sourcing equipment. The consuming end is the PD, powered device. Both terms appear in switch output and on equipment datasheets.

A PoE-capable switch does not simply energize its ports. Doing so would put voltage on a cable that might be connected to something that cannot survive it. Instead the switch tests each port before supplying anything.

  1. Detection. The switch applies a small voltage and looks for a specific electrical signature that a powered device presents. No signature, no power — which is what protects a laptop plugged into a PoE port.
  2. Classification. Having found a powered device, the switch asks how much power it needs. The device answers with a class, and the switch reserves that much from its budget.
  3. Delivery. The switch supplies power, and keeps checking that the device is still there. Unplug the device and the port stops supplying within moments.

All three steps happen in under a second, with no involvement from you beyond having allowed them.

By default a PoE-capable port runs that check on whatever is connected and powers anything that passes it. The command below does not switch power on; it states that default, changes it, or withdraws it.

The command, and its three settings

power inline auto
Switch(config)# interface gigabitethernet 1/0/12
Switch(config-if)# power inline auto
Switch(config-if)# end
Switch#

auto is one of three settings the command accepts, and all three are in the table below. It is also the default, so on a port nobody has changed you would not need to type this at all. In the full sequence this command sits with the other port settings, between switchport access vlan 20 and no shutdown.

The three settings power inline accepts.
SettingWhat the switch doesWhen it is used
autoDetects, classifies, and supplies what the device asks for, up to the port maximum. The default on a PoE-capable portNearly always. It is the normal setting for a port serving a powered device

What typing auto actually clears

On a port nobody has changed, this command changes nothing and leaves nothing in the configuration.

On a port somebody set to power inline never, it removes that setting and restores detection. A device on a never port looks dead, so typing auto rules that out first.

Classes and the budget

Power over Ethernet is defined by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE). Two of its standards set the limits on the equipment in this lesson: 802.3af, the original, and 802.3at, which raised the power a port may supply. A later standard, 802.3bt, raises it further still on newer switches, into the tens of watts. Which one a switch supports sets the ceiling for every port on it.

The class the device reports during classification tells the switch how much to reserve. The numbers matter for two decisions: whether a given port can power a given device, and whether the switch as a whole can power all of them.

Power classes, as the switch reserves them at the port.
ClassReserved at the portTypical device
015.4 WA device that reports no class. The switch reserves the full amount rather than guess
14.0 WLow-draw devices such as some sensors
27.0 WBasic desk phones, simple cameras
315.4 WWireless access points, pan-tilt-zoom cameras, feature phones
430.0 WHigher-draw devices, and only on a switch that supports 802.3at

Two separate limits apply, and confusing them leads to the wrong diagnosis.

  • The per-port limit is what one port can supply. A switch supporting 802.3af supplies up to 15.4 W per port, which covers classes 0 through 3. A class 4 device on such a port will not power up, and no configuration changes that.
  • The switch budget is the total the switch has to share across all of its ports, and it is far less than the per-port limit multiplied by the port count. A switch with a 370 W budget cannot supply 15.4 W to forty-eight ports at once. It supplies what it has, in the order the ports were allocated, and refuses the rest.
A budget that runs out looks like a cabling fault

When the switch has no power left to allocate, the next device connected does not power up. There is no alarm at the desk and nothing visibly different about that port. The technician standing at the far end sees a device that will not start on a cable that tests good, and starts re-terminating a jack that was never the problem.

The switch knows, and says so plainly when asked. That is the reason power is checked at the switch rather than inferred from the device.

Power and data are independent

A link can carry data with no power, and a device can be powered on a link carrying no useful data. The two are configured by different commands and confirmed by different commands.

Keeping them separate gives you two facts instead of one when something goes wrong: a device that is dark has a power problem, while a device that lights up and reaches nothing has a data problem. Those lead in opposite directions.

The configuration, complete

That is the last of the configuration commands. Assembled in order, they are the whole job.

529.3.7 Reading the verification commands

Typing a command without an error message proves that the switch accepted it. Five commands prove that it worked.

A configuration command that produces no error has been accepted, and that is all it has done. It does not tell you that the VLAN exists under the name you meant, that the port came up, that the device on the far end is drawing power, or that the cable between them is carrying anything.

The verification commands are all typed at Switch#. None of them changes anything, so none of them can make matters worse. Run all five and read what each one returns before you draw a conclusion. A single command answering the way you hoped is the fastest route to a wrong diagnosis.

One — is the VLAN there, and is it named

show vlan brief
Switch# show vlan brief

VLAN Name                             Status    Ports
---- -------------------------------- --------- -------------------------------
1    default                          active    Gi1/0/1, Gi1/0/2, Gi1/0/3
20   DATA-20                          active    Gi1/0/12
30   CAMERA-30                        active    Gi1/0/20, Gi1/0/21

Three facts in one screen. VLAN 20 exists. It is named DATA-20. Port Gi1/0/12 is listed against it, so the assignment took. A VLAN listed with no ports beside it was created and never used; a port still sitting in VLAN 1 was never moved. Status reads active, which is the normal state for a VLAN the switch is using.

Two — is the port up, in the right VLAN, and at what speed

show interfaces status
Switch# show interfaces status

Port      Name          Status       Vlan    Duplex  Speed Type
Gi1/0/11                notconnect   1         auto   auto 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/12                connected    20        a-full a-1000 10/100/1000BaseTX
Gi1/0/13                disabled     1         auto   auto 10/100/1000BaseTX

Gi1/0/12 reads connected, in VLAN 20, at a-full and a-1000 — full duplex, meaning the link carries traffic both ways at the same time, and 1000 Mbps. The a- on each means both were negotiated between the two ends rather than forced by a setting. Compare the two neighbors: notconnect means nothing is plugged in or the link is not coming up, and disabled means somebody shut the port down.

Three status words that mean different things
  • connected — a device is on the far end and a link has formed. That rules out an open or a dead run, but not a marginal one. Read the Speed column before you clear the cabling.
  • notconnect — the port is enabled but no link is forming. Nothing plugged in, a cord not seated, a fault in the run, or a device that is off.
  • disabled — the port is shut down in the configuration. Nothing on the far end will ever make it link. no shutdown is the fix.

That is what the Speed column is for. A gigabit-capable device on a gigabit-capable port that negotiates a-100 is reporting a real problem — commonly a run with a fault on a pair that only the higher speed uses. The link works, so nobody reports it, and it stays that way for years.

Three — is the device drawing power

show power inline
Switch# show power inline

Available:370.0(w)  Used:19.4(w)  Remaining:350.6(w)

Interface Admin  Oper       Power   Device              Class Max
                            (Watts)
--------- ------ ---------- ------- ------------------- ----- ----
Gi1/0/11  auto   off        0.0     n/a                 n/a   30.0
Gi1/0/12  auto   on         15.4    Ieee PD             3     30.0
Gi1/0/13  never  off        0.0     n/a                 n/a   30.0

The header line is the switch budget: 370 W available, 19.4 W in use, 350.6 W remaining — the whole switch, so it counts powered ports beyond the three shown here. On Gi1/0/12: Admin auto — power is permitted. Oper on — it is flowing. 15.4 W — what the switch has set aside, not a measurement of what the device is pulling. Class 3 — what the device asked for, which is where that figure comes from. Max 30.0 — the ceiling of the port, not of the device. Device reads Ieee PD, meaning the switch identified a standards-compliant powered device and nothing more about it; where a device announces itself over a discovery protocol, its name appears here instead. Note Gi1/0/13: Admin never, so that port will not power anything regardless of what is connected to it.

Admin and Oper are two different answers

Admin is what the configuration permits. Oper is what is actually happening. Reading them together separates two faults that look identical from the far end.

Admin never, Oper off is a configuration fault, and one command fixes it. Admin auto, Oper off means power is permitted and the switch is still not supplying it — so either nothing is detected on the far end, or there is no budget left to allocate. Those are different problems and the header line tells you which.

Four — is the device actually on your port

show mac address-table interface
Switch# show mac address-table interface gigabitethernet 1/0/12

          Mac Address Table
-------------------------------------------

Vlan    Mac Address       Type        Ports
----    -----------       --------    -----
  20    7483.c21a.9f04    DYNAMIC     Gi1/0/12

The switch has heard from a device with the Media Access Control (MAC) address 7483.c21a.9f04, on port Gi1/0/12, in VLAN 20. That is the device on the far end of the run, its port, and its VLAN, all confirmed from the switch rather than assumed. Type reads DYNAMIC because the switch learned that MAC address from a frame rather than being told it.

A MAC address in that table was put there by the device at the far end — not by you, and not by the switch’s own settings. A MAC address appearing here is proof that a real device on the far end sent a real frame, that the frame crossed every element of the cabling in between, and that the switch placed it in the VLAN you configured. One line of output confirms the physical path and the VLAN assignment together.

An empty result is also an answer

No entry means the switch has heard nothing on that port recently — a MAC address is dropped from the table after a few minutes of silence. The device is off, nothing is connected, or the run is not carrying frames. All three of those sit below the configuration you just made.

That makes it the command that decides where to look next. A MAC address present sends you upward, toward IP addressing and reachability. A MAC address absent sends you back down, to the cord, the run, and the device itself — or, where the port is already up and supplying power, to the device alone.

Five — does the port configuration match the work order

show running-config interface
Switch# show running-config interface gigabitethernet 1/0/12

Building configuration...

Current configuration : 90 bytes
!
interface GigabitEthernet1/0/12
 switchport access vlan 20
 switchport mode access
end

Everything the switch holds for this one port, and nothing else. The first two lines are the switch reporting that it is assembling the answer and how long the answer is; the closing end is where the configuration for that port stops, printed here rather than typed. A configuration records what has been changed from the default, not every option available. Note what is not printed: there is no shutdown line, and its absence is how you confirm the port is enabled. There is no power inline line either, because auto is the default — power is confirmed with show power inline instead.

Read this one last and read it against the work order rather than against your memory of what you typed. It is the check that catches the port configured correctly at the wrong number.

529.3.8 Two configurations, and why the difference matters

The configuration you typed is already in effect, and it is not saved. Both are true at the same time.

A switch holds two copies of its configuration, in two different kinds of memory, and they can differ from each other.

The two configurations a switch holds.
Running configurationStartup configuration
Where it livesWorking memoryNon-volatile memory
What it isWhat the switch is doing right nowWhat the switch will do after it next restarts
When it changesThe instant you press Enter on a configuration lineOnly when you deliberately save
On loss of powerGoneKept
How to read itshow running-configshow startup-config

Every configuration command you typed went into the running configuration and took effect immediately. None of them went into the startup configuration. A switch that loses power at this point comes back with the port in VLAN 1. The VLAN you created normally survives, for the reason the note below gives, but nothing on the port says it was ever meant to be in it.

The VLAN usually survives; the port configuration does not

On this equipment the list of VLANs is held in its own file in flash, separate from the configuration. VLAN 20 and its name are normally still there after a restart. The port assignment is not.

That is the confusing part of the failure. show vlan brief looks half right — the VLAN exists — but the port is sitting in VLAN 1, so nothing works and the obvious check does not show why.

The failure this produces is the confusing kind

Unsaved work does not fail while you are standing there. It fails weeks later, at a power cut or a scheduled restart, and it fails on a port that has been working since the day it was installed.

Nothing connects the symptom to the cause. The paperwork says the port was configured. The port map says VLAN 20. The switch says VLAN 1, and the last person to touch it left no trace because the trace was never written down. Somebody will spend an afternoon on it.

Saving

Copying the running configuration into the startup configuration
Switch# copy running-config startup-config
Destination filename [startup-config]?
Building configuration...
[OK]

The switch asks for the destination and offers the answer in square brackets. Pressing Enter accepts it. [OK] is the confirmation that the save completed — wait for it rather than assuming it.

Read the command as a sentence and it is hard to get backward: copy the running configuration into the startup configuration. Reversing it pulls the saved configuration back into the running one and merges the two. It is rarely what anybody wants, and it is not an undo. write memory, often typed as wr, does the same job as the forward version and appears in plenty of documentation; either is correct.

Where the save belongs in your sequence

Save after verifying, not before. Verification can send you back to change something, and saving a configuration you have not yet confirmed just preserves whatever is wrong with it.

The order is: configure, verify all five, correct anything the verification found, then save. The save is the last thing you do at the switch.

Checking that a save actually happened

To confirm your own settings survived the save, read show startup-config and look for them. Where they appear, the save took.

Some switches also warn you, when somebody types reload to restart them, that the configuration has been modified, and offer to save it first. Treat that prompt as a reminder rather than a safety net. It appears only when somebody is restarting the switch on purpose, and a power cut does not ask.

529.3.9 Where your scope ends

The configuration sequence is a complete job. The list below is what sits immediately beyond it, written down so you can recognize it rather than wander into it.

A managed switch accepts far more commands than the sequence you have just learned. Some of them change how the whole building behaves, and some do so without producing an error message at the time.

The boundary is not about capability, and it is not a comment on what you are able to learn. It is about who carries the consequence. A port assignment affects one desk. A change to how switches talk to each other affects everybody, and the person who has to answer for that is the person whose job it is.

Recognizing a task as being outside your scope and handing it over with evidence is a correct outcome, not a failure to complete the work. It also takes more knowledge than it looks, because it means knowing the names of things you are never going to configure.

Why the list is written down rather than left implied

An unwritten boundary gets crossed by accident, usually by somebody helpful. Somebody at the site will eventually ask you to do one of these, and they will ask reasonably, because you are the person standing next to the switch with a console cable in your hand.

Having the list means you can answer specifically instead of vaguely: configuring a trunk is not something I do — I can raise it with the network team today and give them the port and the switch. That is a better answer than declining, and a far better answer than trying.

The one IP addressing rule you do need

IP address arithmetic sits outside your scope. One rule about IP addressing sits inside it, because without that rule you cannot tell whether a device that reaches nothing is your problem or the network team’s.

The rule, in three sentences

In a correctly built network, one VLAN carries one IP address range. Two devices in the same VLAN, with addresses in that range, reach each other directly. Two devices in different VLANs do not, and connecting them takes a router — whether that is permitted is somebody else’s decision.

If you find the same IP address range in use in two different VLANs, that is a design error rather than something a router will resolve. Report it.

That rule is also a diagnostic tool, and it sorts a large number of complaints. A device that cannot reach anything at all, on a port you have verified as up and in the right VLAN, is a device with an IP addressing problem — observe it, report it, and hand it on. A device that reaches everything on its own group but nothing beyond it is describing the boundary between VLANs working as designed. Neither is repaired by touching the cabling, and knowing that saves the afternoon that would otherwise be spent proving the cable is fine.

What makes an escalation useful

An escalation is only useful if the next person can act on it. The network is broken tells them nothing they did not already know, so they go back to the desk and repeat every check you have already made. Give them your readings instead and they can skip all of that and begin where you stopped.

  • What you verified, with the readings. Port state, VLAN, negotiated speed and duplex, the class and the power reserved, whether the device MAC address was learned. Facts from the switch, not impressions.
  • What you found that you could not resolve. Stated as an observation rather than a theory.
  • Where you stopped, and why. Naming the boundary tells the reader what has been ruled out.
  • What you did not change. As useful as what you did. It tells them the state they are inheriting.
The same finding, twice

“It still does not work, must be a network problem.”

“Port Gi1/0/12 is up at 1 Gbps full duplex, in VLAN 20, class 3 with 15.4 W set aside, and the device MAC is learned on the port. The device holds an IP address and does not answer a ping from a laptop in the same group. Nothing changed on the switch beyond the port configuration on the work order. Handing over at IP addressing and above.”

The second version does three things the first does not: it rules out the physical path with evidence, it names where the boundary was reached, and it leaves the next person a starting point rather than a starting over.

Lesson 529.3 · Section 4 Wrapping Up

529.3.10 What you covered

Configure the port, prove it from the switch, save it, and know where to stop.

Until its ports are assigned to VLANs, a switch keeps them all in one group, so a broadcast arriving anywhere goes everywhere. A VLAN divides those ports into groups that behave like separate switches: ports in one group exchange frames and hear each other’s broadcasts, ports in another group do the same among themselves, and nothing passes between the two groups. It is a setting rather than a piece of wiring, which means it is invisible from the jack, the cord, and the panel. To find out which VLAN a port is in, ask the switch or read the port map.

The console port is how you reach the switch’s command line. It carries no network traffic and needs no IP addressing, so it keeps working on a switch whose network settings are wrong or whose ports are all shut down, and it is the only way to watch the switch start up. Terminal settings are 9600 8-N-1 with flow control off; unreadable characters mean the two ends disagree on how to frame them, usually the speed, and no characters mean the cable, the port choice, or the driver.

The command line is a set of modes, and the prompt states which one you are in. Switch> is look-only, Switch# reads and saves, Switch(config)# changes the whole switch, and Switch(config-if)# changes the one port you named. exit steps back one level; end returns to Switch# from anywhere.

An interface name carries the port type, the stack member, the module, and the port number. The type is set by the hardware and varies between switches in the same rack, so it is confirmed with show interfaces status on the switch in front of you rather than assumed from the last one.

Configure

configure terminal

  name DATA-20
  exit
 interface gigabitethernet 1/0/12   ! or fastethernet, per the switch
  switchport mode access
  switchport access vlan 20
  power inline auto
  no shutdown
  end

Port number and VLAN number come from the work order. The interface type comes from the switch.

Verify — run all five before concluding anything
show vlan brief                        ! is the VLAN there, named, with the port in it
show interfaces status                   ! is the port up, in the VLAN, at speed and duplex
show power inline                        ! is power permitted, flowing, what class, how much reserved

Save — last, and only after verifying
copy running-config startup-config

Configuration commands take effect immediately and are not kept. Until this is typed and [OK] comes back, the next power cut returns the port to VLAN 1.

On Power over Ethernet, the switch detects a powered device, classifies how much it needs, reserves that from a shared budget, and supplies it. power inline auto permits all of that; never forbids it, and a device on such a port looks dead from the far end. Read Admin and Oper together: never and off is a configuration fault, while auto and off means nothing was detected or the budget is exhausted.

The switch’s MAC address table is what decides which direction to look next. A MAC address learned on your port proves a real device sent a real frame across every element of the path and landed in the VLAN you configured.

Where this stops

Subnet calculation, trunks and tagging, Spanning Tree and port security, inter-VLAN routing and gateways, DHCP, stacking and inter-switch protocols, wireless controller configuration, and software or password recovery all sit outside a low-voltage technician’s scope on this equipment.

One rule about IP addressing sits inside it: one VLAN carries one IP address range; same VLAN and same range, they talk; different VLANs need a router. That sentence is the escalation rule.

Each of the five verification commands produces a reading, and a reading is what turns a hand-off from an opinion into a starting point. Configuring a port is the short part. Being able to say what the port is doing, in numbers somebody else can check, is the part that makes the work worth inheriting.
